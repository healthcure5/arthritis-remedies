Warm hands

This month is all about your hands: warmth before you use them, a short routine that grows a little each week, and easier ways to do the jobs that hurt most.

Your four weeks

  1. Week 1: Warm up, then move. Two minutes of warm water, then the five-move hand routine.
  2. Week 2: Add thumb strength. The routine, plus ten thumb presses into a rolled-up sock.
  3. Week 3: Add wrist turns. The routine, the thumb presses, plus ten slow wrist turns each way.
  4. Week 4: Make it yours. Your full routine, every morning, at your own pace.

The warm hands routine

  1. Warm your hands in a bowl of warm water for two minutes (warm, never hot; test it with your wrist).
  2. Finger spread: open your hand as wide as it goes, hold for three seconds, then relax. Five times.
  3. Loose fist: curl your fingers into a soft fist, thumb on the outside, then open. Five times.
  4. Thumb to fingertips: touch your thumb to the tip of each finger, one by one, like counting. Twice through.
  5. Tabletop: keep your fingers straight and bend only at the big knuckles, so your hand makes a table shape. Five times.
  6. Finger walk: rest your hand flat on the table and walk your fingers slowly toward your thumb, one at a time. Twice.

Move slowly. A light stretch is fine. Stop if anything feels sharp, and skip any joint that is hot, red or very swollen.

This month's Sunday emails

Week 1Your hands this month

This month in the Club is about your hands. Knees get most of the attention, but you can rest a sore knee for an afternoon. You can't rest your hands.

So can a few minutes of hand exercise really make a difference? A team in Norway tested it. They took 80 women with hand osteoarthritis and split them into two groups. Half got information only. Half did a short hand routine at home: a warm-up, finger stretches, and exercises for the thumb and grip. Over three months they did it about 37 times, for around 24 minutes each time.

After three months, the exercise group had a stronger grip in both hands, less joint pain and an easier time with everyday tasks than the information group. Their fingers also kept their movement, while the other group's got a little stiffer.

It was a small study, shared at the American College of Rheumatology's 2013 meeting, so it isn't the final word. But it fits what larger reviews have found: hand exercise helps a little, it's safe, and it's easy to keep up because you can do it sitting down.

Source: Hennig, Kjeken et al., Norway, presented at the ACR/ARHP Annual Meeting (2013)

Is it true?

Should you rest a sore hand completely?

Usually not. Long rest lets joints stiffen and muscles weaken. Most arthritis groups suggest gentle movement every day, with lighter jobs and short breaks on bad days. A hot, red, very swollen joint is different: rest it and call your doctor.

Verdict: Move it gently, pace the hard jobs.

Recipe: Black bean and sweet potato chili

Makes 4 bowls · 35 minutes

A warm bowl to hold on a cold night, with frozen diced sweet potato so there's no peeling and no chopping.

  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 bag frozen chopped onion and pepper mix (about 12 oz)
  • 1 bag frozen diced sweet potato (about 12 oz)
  • 2 cans black beans, drained and rinsed
  • 1 can (14 oz) diced tomatoes
  • 1 cup low-sodium vegetable broth
  • 2 teaspoons chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • Salt and pepper
  1. Warm the oil in a large pot over medium heat. Add the onion and pepper and cook for 5 minutes.
  2. Stir in the chili powder and cumin for a minute.
  3. Add the sweet potato, beans, tomatoes and broth. Bring to a boil, then simmer for about 20 minutes, until the sweet potato is soft.
  4. Season with salt and pepper. Freeze extra bowls for a hard day.

Week 2Do arthritis gloves help?

You've probably seen arthritis gloves: snug, fingerless gloves sold to ease hand pain and stiffness. They're comfortable, and some people swear by them. A team at the University of Salford in England tested them properly.

They recruited 206 people with rheumatoid arthritis or a similar inflammatory arthritis and ongoing hand pain. Half got snug arthritis compression gloves. The other half got loose-fitting gloves that looked similar but didn't squeeze. Nobody was told which kind was the 'real' one.

After 12 weeks, both groups said their hand pain was a little better, by about one point on a ten-point scale. There was no real difference between the snug gloves and the loose ones, and no difference in stiffness or how well people could use their hands. The study was published in BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders in 2021, and the researchers advised the UK's health service to stop handing the gloves out routinely.

So if you own a pair and they feel nice and warm, keep wearing them. Warmth is pleasant. Just don't spend much on new ones expecting the squeeze itself to fix anything.

Source: Hammond et al., BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders (2021)

Is it true?

Do arthritis gloves help?

In a 2021 trial of 206 people, snug compression gloves did no better than loose gloves for pain, stiffness or hand function.

Verdict: Keep a pair if you like the warmth, but don't expect the squeeze to help.

Recipe: Ginger carrot soup

Makes 4 bowls · 30 minutes

Bright, warming and smooth, made with frozen sliced carrots and a jar of ready-minced ginger.

  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 bag frozen chopped onion (about 10 oz)
  • 1 bag frozen sliced carrots (about 16 oz)
  • 1 tablespoon jarred minced ginger
  • 4 cups low-sodium vegetable broth
  • 1/2 cup canned coconut milk
  • Salt and pepper
  1. Warm the oil in a pot and cook the onion for 5 minutes.
  2. Add the carrots, ginger and broth. Simmer for 20 minutes, until the carrots are very soft.
  3. Let it cool a little, then blend until smooth. A stick blender saves lifting a hot pot.
  4. Stir in the coconut milk and season to taste.

Week 3What really helped sore thumbs

The joint at the base of the thumb is one of the most common places for hand arthritis. It aches when you turn a key, open a jar or pinch something small. Thumb splints are often handed out for it, so a UK team decided to test whether they add anything.

They enrolled 349 adults with thumb-base arthritis at 17 NHS hospitals and split them into three groups. Everyone got a self-management program from a therapist: how to protect the joint, exercises, and easier ways to do daily jobs. One group also got a real thumb splint, one got a fake splint, and one got no splint at all.

All three groups improved. And the splint made no extra difference to hand pain after eight weeks. The study was published in the journal Rheumatology in 2021.

Here's the part worth holding onto: the people got better from the self-management program itself, the exercises and the smarter ways of doing things. That's exactly what this month's routine and weekend challenges are. If a splint helps you, keep using it. But the daily habits are doing the heavy lifting.

Source: Adams et al., Rheumatology (2021)

Is it true?

Does a thumb splint help thumb arthritis?

In a 2021 trial of 349 people, adding a splint to a self-management program made no extra difference to hand pain. The program itself is what helped.

Verdict: Fine if you like it, but the daily habits matter more.

Week 4A month of warm hands

This is the last week of our hands month, so let's look back.

Four weeks ago you started with two minutes of warm water and five small moves. Since then you've added thumb presses and wrist turns, made one kitchen job easier and found a new way around your hardest hand task.

Here's what this month's three studies add up to. In Norway, a short daily hand routine meant a stronger grip and less pain. In Salford, the gloves did no better than plain loose ones. And in the thumb trial, the splint added nothing, because the self-management habits did the work.

So the things that helped were the ones you've been doing: warmth, small daily movement and smarter ways of doing hard jobs. None of them cost much, which is part of why so few people hear about them.

Keep your routine going after this month, even on the days you skip everything else. Next month we move on to your legs and knees, and your hands come along for the ride.

RoutineOne minute before you get out of bed

Most people start something new by doing everything at once, and by week two it's gone. So start with one minute a day, on the edge of the bed, before your feet touch the floor. It may feel too easy. Good: the goal right now is a habit you do without thinking. A University College London study found new daily habits took about 66 days, on average, to feel automatic.

  1. Ankle pumps. Lift your toes, then your heels. Rock back and forth for 30 seconds.
  2. Hand opens. Spread your fingers wide, then make a loose fist. Ten times.
  3. Tick your calendar each day you do it.

On mornings you feel good, add heel slides (slide one foot forward along the floor and back, five each side) and five slow shoulder rolls.

Move slowly. A light pull is fine. Stop if anything feels sharp.

RecipeOvernight oats with berries and walnuts

Some mornings a jar lid feels like an argument you're going to lose. Make breakfast the night before. Use a container with a wide lid, or rest the lid on top instead of screwing it on. Oats, berries and walnuts turn up often in Mediterranean-style eating, which several studies have linked with less joint pain.

Makes 1 serving · 5 minutes the night before

You'll need

  • 1/2 cup rolled oats
  • 1/2 cup unsweetened soy, oat or almond milk
  • 1/4 cup plain plant yogurt (soy or coconut)
  • 1/2 cup berries (frozen is fine and cheaper)
  • 1 tablespoon chopped walnuts
  • A pinch of cinnamon

How to make it

  1. Stir the oats, milk, yogurt and cinnamon together in a jar or bowl.
  2. Cover and put it in the fridge overnight.
  3. In the morning, add the berries and walnuts. Eat it cold, or warm it for a minute in the microwave.

Forgot to make it? Spoon plant yogurt into a bowl and top it with berries and a few walnuts. Breakfast in one minute.

Weekend challengeThe bowl of warm water trick

Ask people with stiff hands what helps first thing, and one answer comes up again and again: warm water. Warmth relaxes the muscles around a joint, and the first moves of the day often feel easier afterwards. Missed a day? That's normal. The one rule to keep: never miss two days in a row.

  1. Saturday and Sunday morning, rest your hands in a bowl of warm water for two minutes, slowly opening and closing your fingers. A warm shower works too.
  2. Then sit on the edge of the bed and do your one-minute routine.
  3. Tick both days on your calendar.

Warm, never hot. Test the water with your wrist first.

RoutineSame minute, plus sit-to-stand

Once the bed minute happens without much thought, add one move: sit-to-stand, getting up from a chair on purpose a few times in a row. Strong legs take some of the load off your knees and hips. How to tell if you did too much: the Arthritis Foundation's two-hour rule. If your pain is worse two hours after moving than before, do a little less next time. Ease off, don't stop.

  1. Ankle pumps and hand opens on the edge of the bed.
  2. Sit-to-stand. Scoot forward, feet flat, lean your nose over your toes and stand up. Use your hands on the bed or a sturdy chair if you need to. Sit back down slowly. Three times.
  3. Put a tick on your calendar each day you do it.

Move slowly. A light pull is fine. Stop if anything feels sharp.

RecipeSheet-pan chickpeas and vegetables

If your knuckles ache just looking at a whole head of broccoli, this one's for you. One pan, a bag of vegetables someone else already cut, and foil so there's nothing to scrub. Beans and vegetables come up again and again in research on eating for your joints.

Serves 2 · about 30 minutes

You'll need

  • 1 can chickpeas, drained and patted dry
  • 1 bag pre-cut vegetables, such as broccoli and peppers (about 12 oz)
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 lemon, cut in half
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • Salt and pepper

How to make it

  1. Heat the oven to 400°F. Line a baking sheet with foil or parchment.
  2. Spread the chickpeas and vegetables on the sheet. Drizzle with the oil and sprinkle with garlic powder, paprika, salt and pepper.
  3. Bake for 25 to 30 minutes, giving the pan a shake halfway, until the chickpeas are crisp at the edges.
  4. Squeeze lemon over everything.

RoutineMorning moves, plus a five-minute walk

Here's how you know a habit is taking hold: you sit on the edge of the bed and your feet start pumping before you've decided to. When that happens, you're ready for a short walk. Around the block, up and down the hallway, a lap of the grocery store. It all counts. The Arthritis Foundation built a whole program on this idea, called Walk With Ease: start short, go a little further each week.

  1. Bed minute and three stands, same as before.
  2. A five-minute walk sometime during the day. Wear shoes with good support and stay on flat ground.
  3. Tick the calendar for the moves, and add a second tick for the walk.

Move slowly. A light pull is fine. Stop if anything feels sharp.

RecipeRed lentil and vegetable soup

Some days, standing at the stove for half an hour just isn't going to happen. This soup is for those days. You make it on a good one. Frozen chopped onion, carrot and celery mean there's nothing to chop. It makes about six bowls, so freeze the rest in single portions. Lentils are cheap, filling and keep for months in the cupboard.

Makes about 6 bowls · 35 minutes

You'll need

  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 bag frozen chopped onion, carrot and celery mix (about 12 oz)
  • 1 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1 cup dried red lentils, rinsed
  • 1 can (14 oz) diced tomatoes
  • 4 cups low-sodium vegetable broth
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 2 handfuls fresh spinach
  • Salt and pepper

How to make it

  1. Warm the oil in a large pot over medium heat. Add the frozen vegetables and cook for 5 minutes, stirring now and then.
  2. Add the garlic powder and cumin and stir for a minute.
  3. Add the lentils, tomatoes and broth. Bring to a boil, then turn it down and simmer for about 20 minutes, until the lentils are soft.
  4. Stir in the spinach until it wilts. Add salt and pepper to taste.
  5. Let it cool before you pour it into containers. A ladle and a funnel make this easier on your hands.
Weekend challengeWho could you walk with this weekend?

A walk on your own is easy to skip. A walk with someone is a promise you don't want to break. In a study at Baylor College of Medicine, people over 50 with knee arthritis who walked for exercise were less likely to develop daily knee pain.

  1. Keep your morning moves going on Saturday and Sunday.
  2. Take your five-minute walk on both days, and invite someone along: a neighbor, a grandchild, your partner, or a friend on the phone.
  3. If it feels good, go a little past five minutes. If it doesn't, five is still a win.

If you use a cane or walker, bring it. On a painful day, a slow walk around the house still counts.

RoutineTwo calm minutes at bedtime

You know the nights. A hip complains, and you're wide awake at three in the morning, already dreading how stiff you'll be at seven. That stiffness the next day is real: researchers at Johns Hopkins found that people with knee arthritis who slept poorly tended to feel pain more strongly. Two calm minutes won't fix poor sleep, but they can help your body settle.

  1. Morning: bed minute, three stands and your short walk, same as before.
  2. Night: sit on the edge of the bed. Breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of six, five times. Then roll your shoulders up, back and down, five times.
  3. Give the bedtime habit its own tick on the calendar.

Move slowly. A light pull is fine. Stop if anything feels sharp.

RecipeGolden milk

There's something about holding a warm mug in both hands at the end of a long day. Stiff fingers seem to like it too. Golden milk has no caffeine, so it won't keep you up. A word about turmeric: the teaspoon in this mug is far less than the capsules used in studies. Enjoy it as a cozy drink, not a treatment. If you take a blood thinner, check with your doctor or pharmacist about turmeric first.

Makes 1 mug · 5 minutes

You'll need

  • 1 cup unsweetened soy or oat milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground ginger
  • A pinch of black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon maple syrup (optional)

How to make it

  1. Pour the milk into a small pan and whisk in the turmeric, cinnamon, ginger and pepper.
  2. Warm it over low heat until it steams. Don't let it boil.
  3. Pour it into a mug with a big handle and stir in the maple syrup if you like.

Sunday letterYou're part of a very big crowd

In 2024 the CDC's National Health Interview Survey found that 21.3% of American adults had been told by a doctor they have arthritis. It climbs with age: about 4% of adults 18 to 34, and close to 54% of people 75 and older (CDC, 2022). Researchers in Arthritis & Rheumatology (2016) estimated 78 million American adults will have it by 2040. That makes it one of the most studied health problems in the country, and good news often never reaches the people who need it. The stiffness and the careful way you take the stairs don't mean you did something wrong, and you're not alone.

Is it true?

Is arthritis just part of getting old?

Not quite. It's far more common with age, but about 4% of adults aged 18 to 34 already have it. Age raises the risk. It doesn't explain everything, and it doesn't mean nothing can be done.

Verdict: Partly true, and nowhere near the whole story.

Source: CDC National Health Interview Survey (2024); CDC arthritis data (2022); Arthritis & Rheumatology (2016)

Sunday letterWhat 1,212 people over 50 taught us about walking

A common fear: every step wears the knee out, so walk less. Research says that's mostly backwards. In 2022 Dr. Grace Lo's team at Baylor College of Medicine looked at 1,212 people 50 and older with knee osteoarthritis on X-rays (from the Osteoarthritis Initiative). Among those without daily knee pain yet, people who walked for exercise had 40% lower odds of developing frequent knee pain, and showed less narrowing inside the joint. It didn't make existing daily pain go away; the big finding was prevention. Cartilage has no blood vessels and is fed by joint fluid that moves in and out as the joint is loaded and unloaded, like a sponge. Walking also strengthens the muscles that take load off the knee. If walking hurts now, start shorter, slower or on flatter ground.

Is it true?

Heat or ice for stiff joints?

For everyday stiffness, heat is usually better: a moist heating pad wrapped in a cloth, a warm bath, or a shower between 92 and 100 degrees (Arthritis Foundation). Ice suits a fresh flare, when a joint is swollen or warm.

Verdict: Heat for stiffness, ice for a hot, swollen flare. Never fall asleep on a heating pad, and keep a cloth between ice and skin.

Source: Lo et al., Arthritis & Rheumatology (2022)

Sunday letterFour pounds off your knees for every one you lose

In 2005 Stephen Messier's team at Wake Forest University followed 142 adults over 60 with knee osteoarthritis and extra weight. Over 18 months they lost about 2% of their body weight on average. For every pound lost, the load on the knee dropped by about four pounds with each step. Multiply that by every step in a day and a small change adds up. Later work (Arthritis Care & Research, 2016) found more weight lost tended to mean more pain relief, and The Lancet Rheumatology (2023) estimated high body weight is behind about 20% of osteoarthritis disability. Fat tissue also releases chemicals that feed inflammation, which may be why weight is linked to hand arthritis too. Small, slow changes count far more than most people think. Talk with your doctor about a safe pace.

Is it true?

Do copper bracelets help?

In 2013 the University of York ran the first randomized trial of copper bracelets and magnetic wrist straps for rheumatoid arthritis. Seventy people each wore four devices for five weeks at a time, one of them fake. None of the real ones beat the fake for pain, inflammation or getting around.

Verdict: Save your money.

Source: Messier et al., Arthritis & Rheumatism (2005); University of York trial (2013)

Sunday letterWhy everything hurts more after a bad night

People assumed pain keeps us awake. Johns Hopkins researchers asked whether it also works the other way. In 2015 Claudia Campbell's team studied 208 people with knee osteoarthritis using sleep diaries, wrist monitors and overnight lab tests. Those who also had insomnia showed the most 'central sensitization': the nervous system turns the volume up on pain. People stuck in worried, spiraling thoughts about pain had more of it. The study couldn't prove poor sleep causes worse pain, but the link was strong enough that sleep should be part of treatment. The good news: if part of morning pain is the volume knob turned up, the volume can come back down. Insomnia is common and treatable; a talking therapy built for it, called CBT-I, has a strong record.

Is it true?

Can your joints really feel the weather coming?

Partly. The UK study Cloudy with a Chance of Pain collected over five million daily pain reports from more than 13,000 people. Damp, windy days with low air pressure were linked to more pain; cold alone wasn't. The effect was modest: a 5 in 100 chance of a painful day rose to about 6 in 100.

Verdict: True, but smaller than it feels. And it isn't in your head.

Source: Campbell et al., Arthritis Care & Research (2015); Cloudy with a Chance of Pain (Versus Arthritis)

Sunday letterTai chi went head to head with physical therapy

In 2016 Dr. Chenchen Wang's team at Tufts Medical Center split 204 people with painful knee osteoarthritis into two groups: tai chi classes twice a week for 12 weeks, or standard physical therapy. After 12 weeks both groups had about the same drop in pain and the same gains in function, and the benefits held for a year. The tai chi group also had bigger improvements in depression and quality of life. The people in the study looked like real patients: many older, many carrying extra weight. Slow weight shifts strengthen the muscles around the knee, and the balance work, slow breathing and calm focus work on the person, not just the joint. Movement you enjoy enough to keep doing can be every bit as good.

Many senior centers, YMCAs and community centers run beginner tai chi classes for little or no cost.

Source: Wang et al., Annals of Internal Medicine

Sunday letterAbout the heavy days

The part of arthritis no X-ray shows: canceling plans because of the stairs, needing help with things you've done alone for fifty years, worry late at night. A 2018 CDC report found that among adults with arthritis, 22.5% had symptoms of anxiety and 12.1% had symptoms of depression, both more than twice as common as in adults without arthritis. Pain and mood feed each other: pain makes it harder to sleep, move and see friends, and low mood makes pain feel louder. What helps break the circle: regular physical activity, self-management programs like the Chronic Disease Self-Management Program, and talking with a mental health professional. They work together.

If you're having a really hard time, please tell your doctor. In the US you can call or text 988 any time, day or night. You don't have to be in crisis to call.

Source: CDC, Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (2018)

Sunday letterThe turmeric trial, and what it really showed

In 2020 the University of Tasmania published a trial in the Annals of Internal Medicine: 70 people with painful knee osteoarthritis and swelling inside the knee (confirmed by ultrasound). Half took two capsules a day of a turmeric extract, half took look-alike capsules, and nobody knew which. After 12 weeks the turmeric group had less knee pain and used fewer pain medicines, with no serious side effects. The fine print: it didn't reduce the swelling on MRI or change the cartilage, the effect was moderate, and the trial was small and short. Turmeric extract has decent early evidence for knee pain in people with some joint inflammation. It doesn't repair joints, and kitchen turmeric isn't the same as a standardized extract.

Turmeric supplements can interact with some medicines, including blood thinners. Check with your doctor or pharmacist first.

Source: Wang et al., Annals of Internal Medicine (2020)

Sunday letterMost people with arthritis move less

In 2019 the CDC counted 54.6 million American adults with arthritis, and only 36.2% met the recommended amount of aerobic activity. It makes sense: when something hurts, you protect it. You park closer and skip the dance. But less movement means weaker muscles, joints taking more load, and stiffness setting in faster, so the world slowly gets smaller. The studies in these letters point the other way: walkers had 40% lower odds of new frequent knee pain (Baylor), and tai chi matched physical therapy (Tufts). Walking is the most common activity among active adults with arthritis. Find the smallest amount of movement that feels safe today, then a little more next week.

Is it true?

Does CBD help arthritis?

We don't know yet. The Arthritis Foundation issued CBD guidance in 2019 after a survey found 79% of 2,600 people with arthritis were using it, had used it or were considering it. Animal studies look promising, but quality human studies are missing. It can interact with some arthritis medicines and products vary a lot.

Verdict: Unproven. If you want to try it, talk to your doctor first, and never use it in place of a prescribed medicine.

Source: CDC (2019); Arthritis Foundation CBD guidance (2019)

Sunday letterThe weight-loss shot and sore knees

In October 2024 The New England Journal of Medicine published STEP 9: 407 adults with obesity and moderate knee osteoarthritis, 68 weeks, everyone given diet and activity counseling, and two in three given weekly semaglutide (the medicine in Ozempic and Wegovy). The semaglutide group lost 13.7% of their weight; the placebo group lost 3.2%. Knee pain on a 100-point scale fell 41.7 points with semaglutide and 27.5 points with placebo. The trial was paid for by the drugmaker, more people stopped because of side effects on the drug (6.7% vs 3.0%), and what happens after stopping is an open question. The overlooked part: the placebo group, with only diet and activity support, still improved by 27.5 points. The basics, kept up with support, did a lot of the work.

Semaglutide is a prescription medicine with real benefits and risks. Write your questions down before your next appointment.

Source: Bliddal et al., New England Journal of Medicine (2024)

Sunday letterSurgery or exercise for a worn knee?

Degenerative meniscus tears show up in about 35% of people over 50, almost as often in people with no knee pain. For years many were trimmed away with keyhole surgery. The Dutch ESCAPE trial put that to the test: 321 patients aged 45 to 70, half given surgery and half an exercise-based physical therapy program, followed for five years. Published in JAMA Network Open (2022), knee function improved about 30 points with surgery and 25 with physical therapy, close enough that therapy was judged no worse, with similar arthritis progression. The Norwegian OMEX trial followed 140 people for ten years (British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2025): 23% of the surgery group developed knee osteoarthritis on X-ray versus 20% of the exercise group. Surgery still has a place, especially for knees that lock or catch, but for most worn menisci the advice is exercise first.

If you've been told you need surgery for a degenerative tear, it's fair to ask whether physical therapy is worth trying first, and what signs would mean surgery is the better choice.

Source: ESCAPE trial, JAMA Network Open (2022); OMEX trial, British Journal of Sports Medicine (2025)

Sunday letterVitamin D and rheumatoid arthritis

VITAL, one of the largest supplement trials in the US, enrolled 25,871 adults (average age 67) at Brigham and Women's Hospital. They took vitamin D (2,000 IU a day) or a placebo, and fish oil or a placebo, for about 5.3 years. In 2022 the BMJ reported that the vitamin D group had a 22% lower rate of new autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, polymyalgia rheumatica and psoriasis: 123 cases versus 155. The effect grew after the first two years. This was about preventing new disease in people who didn't have it, not treating arthritis you already have. It matters for families, since autoimmune diseases run in families and older adults make less vitamin D from sunlight.

Is it true?

Glucosamine and chondroitin?

The biggest test, GAIT (New England Journal of Medicine, 2006), included 1,583 people with knee osteoarthritis. Overall, neither supplement, alone or combined, eased pain better than a placebo, and a two-year follow-up found much the same.

Verdict: For most people, probably not worth the money.

Vitamin D can be too much in high doses. Ask your doctor whether a blood test makes sense before you take a supplement.

Source: Hahn et al., BMJ (2022); GAIT, New England Journal of Medicine (2006)

Sunday letterWhat women with rheumatoid arthritis added to their plates

A 2025 trial in Physiotherapy Theory and Practice split women with rheumatoid arthritis into two groups doing the same six-month exercise program. One group also ate Mediterranean-style, heavy on vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans and olive oil, with less red meat and processed food. Both improved, but the group that changed how they ate did better on almost everything: lower pain, less inflammation in blood tests, stronger grip and easier everyday tasks. Six months after support ended, the benefits were still there. It was a small trial in women only, but an older UK study of 130 women found learning to cook this way eased symptoms too. Food is something you choose several times a day.

If you take medicine for rheumatoid arthritis, keep taking it. Food works alongside treatment, never in place of it.

Source: Physiotherapy Theory and Practice (2025)

Sunday letterFor hands that don't work like they used to

You can rest a sore knee for an afternoon. You can't rest your hands. The Lancet Rheumatology (2023) expects hand osteoarthritis cases to rise about 49% between 2020 and 2050. In 2017 a Cochrane review pooled trials of exercise for hand osteoarthritis covering 381 people. Exercise eased hand pain a little (about half a point lower on a 0 to 10 scale), helped finger stiffness and made a small difference to function, with few side effects. The evidence was rated low quality, so it's no miracle. But half a point in a hand you use hundreds of times a day might be the difference between opening the jar yourself and asking for help. Warmth before use, thicker handles, splints and pacing heavy tasks also help; a hand therapist can show you which suit you.

Is it true?

Does cracking your knuckles cause arthritis?

No. X-ray studies of 300 adults and of 215 adults found no more hand osteoarthritis in knuckle crackers. Dr. Donald Unger cracked the knuckles of one hand and not the other for about 60 years and found no arthritis in either (Arthritis & Rheumatism, 1998). The pop is a gas bubble in the joint fluid.

Verdict: A harmless habit.

If a finger joint suddenly becomes hot, red or very swollen, call your doctor rather than waiting it out.

Source: Cochrane review (2017); The Lancet Rheumatology (2023)

Sunday letter595 million people

In August 2023 The Lancet Rheumatology published the biggest count of osteoarthritis ever made, from the Global Burden of Disease study. In 2020, 595 million people had it: 7.6% of everyone on Earth, up 132% since 1990. By 2050 they expect knee cases to rise about 75%, hip about 79% and hand about 49%. The drivers: longer lives, a growing population and more people carrying extra weight, which was behind about 20% of the disability. The authors wrote there's still no effective cure. That doesn't mean nothing helps. A big share of what makes osteoarthritis worse (weight, movement, sleep, food) is within reach, and the things that help are mostly in daily life rather than in a pill bottle.

Osteoarthritis usually starts after 40. If someone you love is in their 40s or 50s, these habits are worth sharing now.

Source: GBD 2021 Osteoarthritis Collaborators, The Lancet Rheumatology (2023)

Sunday letterIt's never too late

Smoking is one of the strongest known risk factors for rheumatoid arthritis. Dr. Jeffrey Sparks's team at Brigham and Women's Hospital used up to 38 years of data on more than 230,000 nurses. In Arthritis Care & Research (2019), women still smoking were 47% more likely to develop rheumatoid arthritis than never-smokers. The hopeful part: risk fell the longer it had been since quitting. Women who quit 30 or more years earlier had a 37% lower risk of the more severe, antibody-positive kind than recent quitters. It was the first study to show that changing a behavior could lower the risk of that form. Quitting at any age counts, and people with rheumatoid arthritis are more likely to succeed when their health team supports them.

In the US, 1-800-QUIT-NOW connects you with free, confidential help to stop smoking.

Source: Liu, Sparks et al., Arthritis Care & Research (2019)

Sunday letterThe 90-minute mark

A number that keeps coming up in arthritis research: 90 minutes of walking a week. It came from a randomized trial in adults with arthritis, where walking about that much lowered pain and improved function, and the Arthritis Foundation built its Walk With Ease program around it. In 2013 the CDC found almost two-thirds of adults with arthritis walked less than that, from 58% in California to 76% in Tennessee. Spread over a week, 90 minutes is about 13 minutes a day: one lap of the block, or two or three short walks. Walk With Ease is a six-week program, in a group or self-guided, that the CDC says reduces pain and fatigue and improves strength, balance and walking pace. If 13 minutes is out of reach, start with five.

You can find Walk With Ease, including the self-guided version, on the Arthritis Foundation's website.

Source: CDC, Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (2013); Arthritis Foundation Walk With Ease

Sunday letterWhen arthritis gets in the way

CDC survey data from 2016 to 2018: about 58.5 million American adults reported arthritis, and 25.7 million of them (43.9%) said it limited their usual activities. Those numbers are made of small, personal losses: kneeling in the garden, getting down on the floor with a grandchild, the whole museum instead of the first bench, dancing at a wedding. Arthritis slowly makes the world smaller, one skipped thing at a time. But study after study in these letters shows people getting some of their lives back: walking, tai chi, exercise before surgery, food, a few pounds off the knees. Not all of it, and not overnight. Choose the one thing you'd most like back, and keep it in mind. That's what all of this is for.

Source: CDC, Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (2019)

Sunday letterSix months later: what the research keeps saying

Put the first six months of letters together and the things that helped most were small, steady and done regularly: walking a little, moving slowly and often, sleeping better, eating in a way that calms inflammation, easing a few pounds off your joints, and not giving up on the parts of life you love. None of it is flashy, and most of it costs nothing, which is exactly why it gets overlooked. The research doesn't see people with arthritis as old or broken. It sees joints that respond to care, a nervous system that can calm down, and a body that keeps healing for decades.

Source: A summary of letters 1 to 17
