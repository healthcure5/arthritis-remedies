Why stairs get so hard with arthritis, and how to take them more safely

Knee pain going down, hips that complain going up, a hand that grips the rail a little too tightly. What stairs ask of your joints, and the habits that make them easier.

The short answer

  • Climbing and descending stairs is one of the first things many people notice when knee or hip osteoarthritis grows.
  • Use a handrail when there is one, face the stairs, and take your time.
  • Lead with your stronger leg going up and your weaker leg going down. Think "up with the good, down with the bad."
  • Walking, water exercise, tai chi and strength work are all activities the Arthritis Foundation lists for osteoarthritis.
  • Pain that is new, sharp or worsening, or a knee that gives way, is worth a doctor's visit.

Stairs used to be nothing. Now you stop at the bottom and size them up like a hill. Maybe it's the knee, maybe the hip, maybe both. Many people say stairs were the first thing that told them something had changed. That's a real signal, and there's a lot you can do about it.

Why stairs ask so much of arthritic joints

NIAMS says that as osteoarthritis progresses, everyday tasks like climbing stairs, sitting down, standing up and gripping objects can become difficult.1 The most affected joints include the knees and hips.1 Stairs ask both joints to take your weight while they bend, so it's no surprise they speak up.

Taking stairs more safely

The Arthritis Foundation gives these stair tips:2

  • Use the handrail when there's one. If you use a cane, keep it on the side away from the rail.
  • Face the stairs. Turning sideways is less stable, especially with no rail.
  • Going up, lead with your stronger leg. Going down, lead with your weaker one.
  • Take your time. Rushing makes stairs harder, and going slowly may ease worry about them too.
  • With a cane: going up, step with the stronger leg first, then the cane, then the weaker leg. Going down, cane first, then the weaker leg, then the stronger leg.

If you use crutches and can't put any weight on your weaker leg, the Foundation says to avoid stairs.2

Making the joints stronger and calmer

Moving more helps the joints that make stairs hard. The Arthritis Foundation lists walking, water exercise, tai chi, strength training and yoga among the activities for people with osteoarthritis, and notes that a physical therapist can plan exercises around your own joints.3 Beginners can split walks into 10-minute pieces.3 The CDC says physical activity can reduce pain and improve function, and that even small amounts count.4

A healthy weight takes pressure off weight-bearing joints. NIAMS lists weight management as a way to ease knee and hip pain.1 The CDC notes that even a 5% loss, about 12 pounds for someone who weighs 250, can help.5

Ask for a physical therapist

A physical therapist can check how you move, teach exercises that fit your joints and advise on whether a cane, brace or shoe insert would help. NIAMS lists braces and orthotics fitted by a health professional as an option, along with canes or walkers for stability.1 The Arthritis Foundation says to see one if pain increases after exercise and lasts more than a day or two.3

Questions people ask

Why does arthritis make stairs so hard?

Stairs make your knees and hips carry your weight while they bend. With osteoarthritis, tasks like climbing stairs can become difficult as the joint changes, according to NIAMS.

Which leg should I lead with on stairs when I have arthritis?

The Arthritis Foundation says to lead with your stronger leg going up and your weaker leg going down, and to use a handrail and take your time.

Why does my knee hurt going down stairs?

Going down puts a lot of load through the knee as it bends. If knee pain on stairs is new or getting worse, a doctor or physical therapist can check what's going on.

Should I avoid stairs if I have knee arthritis?

Not necessarily. Most people keep using stairs, with a rail and a slower pace. Ask your doctor if you have a reason to avoid them, such as a knee that gives way.

What exercises help with stairs?

Walking, water exercise, tai chi and strength training are listed by the Arthritis Foundation for osteoarthritis. A physical therapist can pick moves that fit your joints.
