Joint pain: what it can mean, and when to see a doctor

Aching, stiff, swollen or hot joints have many causes. Here is what the signs can point to, which ones need care quickly, and what to expect at the visit.

The short answer

  • Joint pain has many causes. Arthritis is a common one, but injuries, overuse, infection and other conditions can do it too.
  • How long it lasts, when it is worst and whether the joint is swollen, red or warm all help a doctor sort it out.
  • A hot, swollen joint with a fever needs medical care right away.
  • Joint pain that lasts, keeps coming back or limits what you do is worth a visit.
  • Only a doctor can say what is causing it.

"My knee hurts. What does that mean?" Fair question, and nobody can answer it from a description alone. What this page can do is show you which details matter, which signs need quick care, and how to get ready for the visit.

What the common signs can point to

The Mayo Clinic says arthritis symptoms mainly involve the joints and commonly include pain, stiffness, swelling, redness and decreased range of motion.1 These are clues, not answers. Each can also come from other problems.

  • Pain and stiffness that ease within about 30 minutes of moving: a pattern NIAMS describes for osteoarthritis.2
  • Longer morning stiffness, swelling and warmth in matching joints: a pattern linked with rheumatoid arthritis, which is why it is worth a doctor's look.3
  • A suddenly hot, red, very painful joint, often the big toe: a pattern described for gout.4
  • Back pain that is worse after rest and eases with movement: a clue the Mayo Clinic lists for ankylosing spondylitis.5

For more detail on each, see types of arthritis.

Notes that help a doctor

Before the visit, jot down:

  1. Which joints hurt, and whether it is one side or both
  2. When it is worst: morning, night, after rest, after activity
  3. How long the stiffness lasts when you first get up
  4. Any swelling, redness, warmth or fever
  5. What makes it better or worse
  6. What it stops you doing: stairs, jars, buttons, walking
  7. Your medicines, and any family history of joint disease

When to get care quickly

The Mayo Clinic says to see a doctor for joint pain with swelling, redness, tenderness or warmth, or with fever, and that a hot, inflamed joint with fever needs immediate care.1 The Arthritis Foundation also lists unusual joint pain and swelling with fever among the reasons to go to the emergency room, since it can mean an infected joint.6

When to book a regular visit

Make an appointment if pain lasts more than a few days, keeps returning, or limits daily tasks, or if morning stiffness runs long. Arthritis Foundation guidance suggests symptoms lasting three days or more, or several episodes in a month, are worth a call.6

What to expect at the visit

NIAMS says doctors usually start with your history and an exam, and may add X-rays or MRI, blood tests, or a joint fluid test.2 You may be referred to a rheumatologist, a doctor who specializes in arthritis and related conditions. If a word is new, try the arthritis A to Z.

While you wait

Questions people ask

What does joint pain usually mean?

It can mean arthritis, but also an injury, overuse, infection or another condition. A doctor can tell which from your history, an exam and sometimes tests.

When should I worry about joint pain?

Get care right away for a hot, swollen joint with a fever, or a joint you suddenly can't move. See a doctor soon if pain lasts, returns, or limits daily tasks.

Is joint pain always arthritis?

No. Arthritis is common, but injuries, overuse, infections and other conditions can cause joint pain too.

What should I tell my doctor about joint pain?

Which joints, when it is worst, how long morning stiffness lasts, any swelling, redness, warmth or fever, what helps, and what it stops you doing.

What kind of doctor treats joint pain?

Often your regular doctor first. A rheumatologist specializes in arthritis and related conditions.
