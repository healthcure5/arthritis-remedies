Ankylosing spondylitis: when back pain and stiffness are more than a sore back

Back pain that is worse after rest and eases when you move is a clue worth knowing. Also called axial spondyloarthritis, it often starts in early adulthood. The signs, and when to ask for help.

The short answer

  • Ankylosing spondylitis (AS), also called axial spondyloarthritis, is an inflammatory disease that mainly affects the spine.
  • It usually starts in late adolescence or early adulthood, with low back and hip pain and stiffness worse in the morning or after rest.
  • Pain that wakes you at night, improves with exercise and worsens with rest is a clue.
  • There's no cure, but treatment can reduce symptoms and may slow progression.
  • A red, painful eye with light sensitivity or blurred vision needs an eye specialist right away.

Most back pain is muscle trouble and it settles. A smaller group has a back that's stiffest after rest and eases with movement, often starting young. If that sounds familiar, it's worth knowing the name.

What it is

The Mayo Clinic says ankylosing spondylitis is an inflammatory disease that mainly affects the spine. Over time, inflammation can cause vertebrae to fuse, reducing flexibility and sometimes leading to a hunched posture. There are two forms: AS, which shows on X-ray, and nonradiographic axial spondyloarthritis, diagnosed through symptoms, blood tests and imaging such as MRI.1

Signs

  • Onset usually in early adulthood
  • Back pain and stiffness, mainly in the lower back and hips, often worse in the morning or after inactivity
  • Neck pain and fatigue
  • Eye pain or vision changes, skin rashes or stomach pain in some people
  • Symptoms that flare and then ease1

When to see a doctor

The Mayo Clinic lists slowly worsening low back or buttock pain, and pain that is worse in the morning, wakes you at night, improves with exercise and worsens with rest.1

Get an eye specialist right away for a red, painful eye, light sensitivity or blurry vision, because it can mean eye inflammation.1

Causes and outlook

The exact cause is unknown, though genes play a role. A gene called HLA-B27 greatly raises risk, but many people with the gene never develop AS, and some people with AS don't have it.1 Possible complications include eye inflammation, spinal compression fractures, heart problems and stiff rib joints that make deep breathing harder.1

There's no cure. Treatment aims to reduce symptoms, manage pain and possibly slow progression.1 Staying active is part of care, and a rheumatologist can guide the plan. See our page on exercise and arthritis.
