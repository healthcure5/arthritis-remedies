Before you begin

Lately your joints have been deciding too much of your day. Which stairs you take. Whether you open the jar yourself. How long you can stand at the stove, sit in the car, or hold someone's hand.

You've likely been told a hundred different things about it. Cut this food. Drink that juice. Try this miracle. It's exhausting to hurt, and even more exhausting to not know who to believe.

I wrote this book for one reason: so the day you're having now isn't the day you're having three months from now. What this one does is hand you the steps that turn stiff, careful days into easier ones, and walk you through them until they're yours.

Right now you may feel stiff in the morning, unsure what's safe to try, and a little afraid that things only go one direction from here. That fear is understandable. It is also not the whole story, and the pages ahead will show you why.

By the last page, you'll know exactly what to do each morning, and what you can let go of. You'll have a simple daily routine you can start tomorrow. You'll understand your own condition well enough to ask your doctor better questions. With a few weeks of steady practice, many people find the mornings get easier, the pain gets quieter, and the things they'd stopped doing start to feel possible again.

You'll go from bracing against your body to working with it, and from getting through the day to living it.

Relief today

If you're hurting right now, start here. These are the quickest comforts in this book. Each one takes less than half an hour, and each points to its full page if you want more.

  1. Warm what is stiff. A hot shower, a heat pack or a wheat bag for 15 to 20 minutes. For hands, rest them in a bowl of comfortably warm water. More in Poultices, packs and warmth.
  2. Cool what is swollen. A cold pack wrapped in a thin cloth for 15 minutes on a joint that feels hot and puffy.
  3. Try hot and cold water for hands, wrists, feet and ankles: three minutes in warm water, 30 seconds in cold, three times over, ending on cold.
  4. Rub in cayenne cream. Capsaicin cream from the pharmacy, on the sorest joint, three or four times a day. It stings at first and that fades over a week or two. Wash your hands well afterward.
  5. Move a little. One minute of seated marching, ten ankle circles, ten slow hand openings. Moving feeds the joint and turns pain down. The full set is in A movement guide for sore joints.
  6. Breathe out slowly. Ten slow breaths, with each breath out longer than the breath in. It settles a nervous system that is turning pain up.

Part one · Understand

What's happening inside your joints

You don't need a medical degree to understand your joints. You only need a clear picture, and once you have it, you'll feel more in charge of your body than you have in a long time.

A joint is where two bones meet. The ends of those bones are capped with cartilage, a smooth, springy tissue that lets them glide. The whole joint is wrapped in a thin lining that makes a slippery fluid, a little like oil in a hinge. Muscles and tendons around the joint hold it steady and take up much of the load.

Why movement is medicine

Cartilage has no blood supply of its own. It is fed by the joint fluid, and that fluid only moves in and out of the cartilage when the joint itself moves. Every bend and stretch is a meal for your cartilage. Rest starves it. As Barbara O'Neill, whose talks shaped much of this book, likes to say, movement brings blood, and blood brings what a joint needs. This is why the joints you move often, without forcing them, are the ones that serve you longest.

What goes wrong

In arthritis, something disturbs that system. The cartilage may thin and roughen. The lining may become inflamed and swollen. Crystals may form inside the joint. The result is the same family of symptoms you know well: pain, stiffness, swelling, and a joint that tires quickly.

Why pain is not the same as damage

If you remember one idea from this chapter, make it this one. Pain is a signal made by your nervous system, and it is turned up or down by many things: poor sleep, stress, fear, cold, low mood, and sitting still for too long. It is very common for joints that look worn on an X-ray to feel fine, and for joints that look healthy to hurt. So a painful day does not mean your joints are crumbling. It means your system is sensitive today, and you have more ways to calm it than you may think.

The three levers you hold

Everything that works in this book pulls one of three levers. It lightens the load on the joint, it calms inflammation, or it settles a sensitive nervous system. Movement, sleep and food pull all three at once, which is why you'll meet them again and again.

Know your arthritis

Each kind of arthritis needs something different, so knowing yours is worth more than any remedy. Popular talks often gather them under one cause, such as "acidity" or "toxins". They are really different conditions, and understanding yours puts you back in charge.

You are far from alone. About 53 million American adults, roughly one in five, have been told by a doctor that they have arthritis. Osteoarthritis is by far the most common, affecting more than 32 million. Gout affects about 9 million Americans, mostly men. Rheumatoid arthritis affects around 1.3 to 1.5 million, and women are two to three times more likely to get it than men.

How each one tends to feel

  • Osteoarthritis: stiffness that eases within about half an hour of getting up, pain that builds with use, and joints that may creak or look knobbly.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: morning stiffness lasting more than an hour, warm and swollen joints on both sides of the body, and often deep tiredness.
  • Gout: a single joint that becomes hot, red and exquisitely tender, often overnight, then settles over days.

Other kinds you may hear about

  • Psoriatic arthritis: joint swelling in someone with the skin condition psoriasis. Roughly one in three people with psoriasis develop it. It can affect the spine too.
  • Ankylosing spondylitis: inflammation in the spine, felt as a stiff, aching lower back that is worst in the morning and eases with movement. It most often starts before 45.
  • Lupus: an immune condition that can bring joint pain together with a rash, deep tiredness and problems in other organs. It is far more common in women.
  • Fibromyalgia: widespread muscle aches, poor sleep and tiredness. It feels like arthritis but does not damage the joints.
  • Bursitis and tendinitis: a sore spot next to a joint, such as the shoulder, elbow or hip, often after overuse. It usually settles within days or weeks.
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome: tingling or numbness in the thumb and first fingers, often worse at night, from pressure on a nerve in the wrist.
  • Joint infection: one joint that turns hot, red and swollen, often with fever. This needs a doctor the same day.

Psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis and lupus are driven by the immune system, like rheumatoid arthritis, and are looked after by a rheumatologist. If you aren't sure which kind you have, a diagnosis is the best first step, because the right treatment depends on it.

Two more things worth knowing about rheumatoid arthritis. Women who breastfed, especially for a year or more in total, have been found less likely to develop it. And studies have found that people with rheumatoid arthritis often have lower levels of folate, protein and zinc than others, partly because of their medicines, so a balanced plate and a blood test now and then are worth it.

If yours is rheumatoid arthritis, please hold on to this: the medication is what protects your joints from lasting damage, and the early months matter most. Let the remedies in this book comfort you alongside it.

Starting early, and letting your doctor adjust the medicine at each visit until the joints go quiet, is why so many people now reach what doctors call remission. A Finnish register found that about 69% of people with rheumatoid arthritis were in remission in a recent year. That is a world away from a generation ago, and it is within reach for many people who begin now.

Where in the world people do best

If you've wondered whether the place you live decides your odds, it doesn't. The countries where the most people with rheumatoid arthritis reach remission don't share a climate or a special food. They share early diagnosis and steady treatment. So the part that matters most travels with you: start early, keep moving, and work with your doctor.

Can arthritis be reversed?

For some kinds, yes. For others, the pain and stiffness can turn around even when the joint itself doesn't change. Here is what's possible for each.

Kinds that can be cured or cleared

  • Joint infection: treated early with the right antibiotics, it is usually cured completely.
  • Lyme arthritis: antibiotics clear it in most people.
  • Reactive arthritis, which can follow a gut or urinary infection, often clears up on its own within a few months to a year.

Kinds that can go quiet for years

  • Gout: keeping blood uric acid below 6 mg/dL slowly dissolves the crystals in the joint. Over months to years, flares can stop entirely.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: started early, treatment brings most people to remission, with no active disease. A smaller group, guided by their doctor, later keep that remission on less medicine or none.

Osteoarthritis: turning the pain around

Worn cartilage hasn't been shown to grow back, but how your knee or hip feels can change a great deal. In an 18-month study of older adults with knee osteoarthritis and extra weight, those who lost about a tenth of their weight through diet and exercise cut their knee pain roughly in half. Strong muscles, a lighter load and daily movement are what make that possible.

The a well-known nutrition reference book I drew from for parts of this edition goes further. It says some forms of arthritis can be reversed, and in some cases cured, with the right diet and lifestyle, and that these changes can ease inflammation and pain, stop the breakdown and renew the affected joints. Easing pain and inflammation with food, weight loss and movement is well supported. Renewing worn cartilage is the part studies haven't shown yet, so let it be a hope rather than a promise.

Where these remedies come from

Many of the remedies in this book, such as ginger poultices, castor oil compresses, alternating hot and cold water and "alkaline" eating, were made popular by the natural health speaker Barbara O'Neill. This edition draws directly on two of her talks on arthritis and gout, so you can see what she actually teaches and put the best of it to work.

Who she is

Barbara O'Neill is an Australian natural-health educator. For many years she and her husband, Michael, ran Misty Mountain Health Retreat at Nulla Creek in New South Wales. Guests came for a week or more to learn about food, rest, herbs and poultices, and the week closed with a short chapel gathering on Friday evening, where they could choose a hymn. She became a Christian at 26, and her faith runs through everything she teaches. She believes the body was made with the ability to heal itself when it's given the right conditions.

Her interest started at home. She was a young mother raising a big family in the rainforest country of New South Wales when one of her little daughters kept getting ear infections. By her account, a simple onion poultice was what finally helped. That small win in her own kitchen set the direction for the rest of her life.

Why her story stays with me

Here is why her story has never let go of me. Barbara was in her early twenties when she watched her mother begin to slip away. It started soon after a house move her mother never wanted, sometime in her forties, with a stiffness in the hands that would not lift. Then the hands stopped working. Then the legs. The name for it was rheumatoid arthritis. She was given long-term steroids, anti-inflammatories and gold injections, and still she went downhill, year after year, until a wheelchair held her. She died at 51. Barbara's father believed to the end of his own life that the anger his wife never set down about that move had helped the illness along. In her final weeks a family friend who trusted natural remedies stepped in with fresh juices, a plate full of plants, water, sunshine and slow, patient massage, and for about six weeks the pain loosened its grip.

What stays with me isn't the illness. It's the daughter standing beside that bed. If you've ever watched someone you love hurt and felt there was nothing in your hands to give them, you already know her. She took that helplessness and spent the rest of her life turning it into its opposite: something to do, something to try, something to hold onto. That is the whole reason this book exists. You should never be left with empty hands.

One thing has changed since then, and it changes the ending for you. Barbara's mother was treated more than fifty years ago, before the medicines we have today. Modern rheumatoid arthritis drugs don't only dull the ache. They can slow or stop the damage that once took hands and legs, and that is why her mother's story is far rarer now. And though a heavy heart can keep pain turned up, no feeling causes rheumatoid arthritis. Her mother did not bring it on herself, and neither did you.

What she gets right

Listen closely and much of her talk is simply good sense. Drink water. Sleep well. Eat mostly plants. Ease off sugar. Keep a healthy weight. Move every day. Notice how your body responds. She is right, too, that arthritis often settles in a joint that was injured years before. You will find all of that in this book, because research supports it.

How this book uses her teaching

She is a teacher rather than a medical doctor, and she tells her audiences to watch how their own body responds. This book takes her at her word. Each of her ideas is sorted for you, so you know which ones to start with and which are simply yours to try. A few, mostly about medicines, are best talked over with your doctor first, and the next chapter points those out.

A method for finding what works

Here is a simple routine: look back over your history, notice what your symptoms are telling you, change one habit, then watch how your body responds. "Does it bring relief?" is the test. That is a sound way to try any mild remedy, and it is exactly what your tracker in Part four is for. Just keep your doctor in the loop, because some causes can't be found by feel.

Three ideas from the talks to take to your doctor first

  • "Drugs never cure, they only mask symptoms." For rheumatoid arthritis, modern medicines do more than that: they protect joints from permanent damage. Some talks suggest easing off medication as lifestyle changes take hold. Only do that after speaking with your doctor, who can lower a dose safely as you improve.
  • "Autoimmune disease isn't real." It is. Rheumatoid arthritis shows up in blood tests and scans. It can go quiet for long stretches, which is called remission and is most often reached with treatment.
  • "Castor oil breaks up tumors, bone spurs, scar tissue and calcified joints." There is no evidence for this. Any new lump deserves a doctor, not a compress.

Eight myths, and what's true

You'll hear these from friends, online, and sometimes in a waiting room. Each one can keep you stuck, so here is a plain answer to each.

"Arthritis is just wear and tear from getting old."

Not quite. The NIH says osteoarthritis isn't simply wearing out, and children get arthritis too. Age raises the risk, but it doesn't decide your future.

"Rest your joints. Exercise makes it worse."

Movement lowers pain and disability. The right exercise protects joints, and stillness is what makes them stiffer.

"Nothing can be done."

Even a booklet from 1948 said nine in ten people could benefit. Today there is far more to work with: movement, weight loss, good medicines and the habits in this book.

"Glucosamine rebuilds cartilage."

The largest trials found little effect, and rheumatology guidelines advise against it.

"Diet doesn't matter."

Losing weight clearly helps, diet matters a lot in gout, and plant-rich eating shows promise in small trials.

"Acidic blood dissolves the cartilage in your joints."

Some nutrition books say this, including the reference book I drew from. Your body keeps your blood's acid level in a very narrow range, between 7.35 and 7.45, whatever you eat. If it ever shifted enough to harm cartilage, you would be seriously ill in hospital, not sore at home. Eating plenty of fruit and vegetables is still a good idea, for other reasons covered in the food chapter.

"Natural means safe."

Some supplements harm the liver, clash with medicines, or contain hidden prescription drugs. Tell your pharmacist everything you take.

"A natural program can replace my rheumatoid arthritis medicine."

Arthritis New Zealand, the NHS and the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health all warn that delaying RA medicine risks permanent damage. Natural care belongs beside it.

Part two · Soothe

Food and drink

Food is one of the easiest tools you have, and it works in a simple way: by easing the load on your joints, calming inflammation and looking after your whole body. You don't need to chase acid or toxins to feel the benefit.

Anti-inflammatory eating

May help

Here the popular advice is on solid ground. Filling your plate with vegetables, fruit, beans, nuts and whole grains, and easing off sugar and processed food, is exactly what mainstream guidance suggests too. A Mediterranean-style pattern with olive oil and oily fish has the best support, and the American College of Rheumatology suggests it for people with rheumatoid arthritis. This isn't a passing fad. As far back as 1991, a study in the Lancet followed people with rheumatoid arthritis who moved to a plant-based diet, and found their swollen joints and morning stiffness eased, with the benefit still there a year later. The change is gradual, and it is real. Eating well sits beside your treatment, it doesn't replace it, and the researchers who ran that study said exactly that.

Oily fish

May help

Salmon, sardines, mackerel and herring carry two omega-3 fats in their oil, called EPA and DHA. Your body uses them to make substances that calm inflammation. The same fish give you protein and vitamin D as well. In rheumatoid arthritis, studies of fish oil show a modest easing of joint pain and morning stiffness. Two portions a week is good for your joints and your heart.

If you don't eat fish. Barbara O'Neill eats only plants, and so did the guests at her retreat. For protein she points to legumes, nuts and seeds, which she calls the cleanest-burning protein: lentils, chickpeas, beans, tofu and hummus. In place of fish oil, her oils are plant ones: olive oil for cooking and salads, with fats from avocado, nuts and seeds, and she steers people away from cheap refined seed oils. If you eat that way, add ground flaxseed or flaxseed oil, chia seeds or walnuts each day. They hold a plant omega-3 called ALA. Your body turns only a small share of ALA into EPA and DHA, so fish has the stronger evidence for joints, but both plates are good ones.

Nightshades

You can keep them

You may have heard that people with arthritis should stop potatoes, tomatoes, peppers and eggplant, because of plant chemicals called lectins. Research hasn't found that link, the Arthritis Foundation calls it a myth, and these foods nourish you. People do differ, though, and your own body's response is the real test. So if you suspect a food, leave it out for two weeks, bring it back, and let your body answer. Peeling and deseeding tomatoes and peppers is harmless if you'd like to try it. One exception is worth knowing: in gout, some people do find that tomatoes set off a flare, and one study linked them with higher uric acid, so the two-week test is especially worthwhile for you. Sweet potatoes are not nightshades at all.

Alkaline eating

Keep the food, let go of the worry

If you've felt better eating this way, I believe you. All those vegetables deserve the credit. The idea is that food turns the fluid around your cells and joints acid. Your body guards that fluid as carefully as it guards your blood, and no study has shown that food changes the acidity of a joint. So enjoy the meals, and set down any fear of "acidic" foods.

The 80/20 plate

A popular rule is to fill about 70 to 80% of your plate with "alkaline" foods and keep everything else to 20 or 30%. Set the chemistry aside and this is simply a plant-rich plate, which is good advice.

  • The 80%: leafy greens and other vegetables, fruit, lemon, millet, quinoa, buckwheat, spelt, kamut, einkorn, almonds, Brazil nuts, lima beans, lentils, soy and seeds.
  • The 20%: meat, aged cheese, modern wheat, other grains, other beans and other nuts, with sugar, caffeine and alcohol kept lowest of all.
  • One correction: walnuts, oats and chickpeas usually land on the "acid" side of these lists, and oily fish is often left off altogether. These are some of the best foods there are for your joints and heart. Enjoy them freely.
  • Cooked greens are welcome too. You may be told to eat spinach and chard raw only, because cooking turns them acid. It doesn't. Cooked greens are just as good for you, and often easier to eat in generous amounts.

Carrot, celery and apple juice

Yours to try

A well-known joint juice is about 80% carrot, 10% celery and 10% apple, once or twice a day. It's a pleasant drink and an easy way to take in vegetables. It hasn't been shown to ease joints, clear uric acid or change your body's acidity, and juice carries more sugar and less fiber than whole vegetables, so one small glass is plenty. If you have diabetes, count it as you would any juice.

Lemon water

Yours to try

The juice of a lemon in water each morning is refreshing and harmless, though it won't alkalize you. Rinse with plain water afterwards to protect your teeth.

Water and Celtic salt

The water is the gift

Drinking enough through the day, around eight glasses, is an easy habit, and it helps keep gout flares away.

If you salt your water, use Celtic salt. It is the only salt Barbara O'Neill uses: the gray, slightly damp sea salt from the coast of France. She prefers it because it is unrefined, so it keeps small amounts of magnesium, potassium and calcium that are stripped out of white table salt, and it has no anti-caking agents. A single crystal on the tongue before a glass of water is the amount she means. It has close to the same sodium as table salt, so check with your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, heart trouble or kidney trouble. And if Celtic is the only salt in your kitchen, get your iodine from fish, eggs, dairy or seaweed, because iodized table salt is where most people get theirs.

The sipping trick

If you avoid water because you worry about rushing to the bathroom, add one glass a day and take it a few mouthfuls at a time. If your doctor has set a fluid limit for your heart or kidneys, keep to that.

Sugar and processed food

Less is better

Refined sugar gets a lot of blame, and on the practical point the blame is fair. Sweet drinks, pastries and heavily processed meals make weight harder to manage and tend to leave people feeling more inflamed and tired. You don't have to give them up forever. Swapping one a day is enough to start feeling lighter.

Tea and coffee

You can keep them

Tea sometimes gets blamed for arthritis. There is no good evidence that moderate tea or coffee causes or worsens arthritis, and both count toward your fluids. If caffeine disturbs your sleep, stop after lunch.

Wheat and bread

You can keep them

Modern wheat is often blamed for inflammation, with ancient grains such as spelt, kamut and einkorn suggested in its place. Those are good grains, and the wholegrain form of any wheat is good for you. There is no good evidence that wheat worsens arthritis unless you have celiac disease or a diagnosed sensitivity. If you suspect it, ask your doctor for a celiac test before you cut wheat out, because the test only works while you're still eating it.

Milk, yogurt and cheese

You can keep them

Aged cheese often turns up on "acid" lists. For gout, the evidence points the other way: low-fat milk and yogurt are linked with fewer flares. Keep rich cheese to modest portions for the sake of your weight and heart.

Red and processed meat

Less is better

Eating less red meat helps, though not because of acid. Red meat is rich in purines, which your body breaks down into uric acid, the cause of gout flares. It also carries saturated fat, and processed meats such as bacon, ham and sausages add a lot of refined salt. Meat does give you protein, iron and vitamin B12, so a small portion now and then is fine. Barbara O'Neill's swap is legumes, nuts and seeds, and having lentils or beans in place of meat a few nights a week is an easy way to start. A plate with more fish, beans and vegetables is the pattern with the best support. If you eat no animal foods at all, ask your doctor about vitamin B12.

A big breakfast and a light supper

Yours to try

The old rule is to eat breakfast like a king, lunch like a queen and supper like a pauper. Some small studies suggest that eating more of your food earlier in the day makes weight loss a little easier, and many people sleep better after a light evening meal. If you take medicines with food at night, keep doing so.

Four more foods worth adding

  • Garlic, onions, leeks and asparagus. These are rich in sulfur, which the body uses to build and repair cartilage and connective tissue. In a study of more than 1,000 women twins, those who ate the most of the onion family showed fewer signs of hip osteoarthritis.
  • Fiber every day. Ground flaxseed, oat bran, rice bran, beans and whole grains. In a large US study, people who ate the most fiber were less likely to develop painful knee osteoarthritis. A spoonful of ground flaxseed a day also brings the plant omega-3 fat.
  • Tart cherries. The reference book suggests about 20 a day, and frozen ones are fine. A small trial of tart cherry juice in knee osteoarthritis found a modest easing of pain and stiffness. There's more on cherries and gout below.
  • Fresh pineapple, often. It contains bromelain, the enzyme described in the supplements chapter. Canning and freezing destroy the enzyme, so fresh is the one to choose.

If yours is gout

Food matters more in gout than in any other arthritis, so here is what protects you. The American College of Rheumatology suggests limiting alcohol (beer and spirits most of all), limiting purine-rich foods such as organ meats, red meat and shellfish, and avoiding drinks sweetened with high-fructose corn syrup. Losing excess weight helps too. Cherries are worth a place in your fruit bowl. In a study of more than 600 people with gout, those who ate cherries over a two-day period had about a third fewer flares in the days that followed. A serving was about ten to twelve cherries, and frozen ones count. It isn't proven, but it's a pleasant thing to try. Vitamin C tablets are not recommended. And if flares keep returning, medication that lowers uric acid is what stops them for good.

Finding your own trigger foods

Some people notice that one food seems to set off their joints. Others never find a link. A short, careful test is the only way to know which group you're in.

Even the NHS, which says there's no strong evidence that diet changes rheumatoid arthritis, notes that some people find certain foods make them worse, and that leaving those out for a few weeks can help find them.

Plant-based eating for rheumatoid arthritis

May help

Dr. Neal Barnard's team ran a 2022 trial in 44 adults with rheumatoid arthritis. On a low-fat vegan diet for 16 weeks, the average number of swollen joints fell from 7 to about 3, and people lost about 14 pounds without counting calories. It was a small study, and the group behind it campaigns for plant-based eating, so treat it as promising rather than proven.

The four-week test

This comes from Dr. Barnard's book Foods That Fight Pain.

  1. For four weeks, build meals from foods that rarely cause trouble: oats, rice, quinoa, millet, buckwheat and lentils; cooked greens such as broccoli, kale, spinach and asparagus; cooked carrots, squash and zucchini; and cooked or dried fruit that isn't citrus, such as pears, apricots, blueberries and plums.
  2. Leave out the common triggers completely: dairy, chocolate, eggs, citrus, meat, wheat, nuts and peanuts, tomatoes, onions, corn, apples and bananas. Skip alcohol and caffeine for the four weeks too.
  3. If you feel better, bring one food back every two days, in a good-sized portion, and watch for a flare. Write it in your tracker.
  4. If a food sets off a flare, take it back out and wait at least two weeks before testing it again. Many people find more than one.
Before you start

This is a big change. Check with your doctor first if you have diabetes, are underweight, take blood thinners or have struggled with eating in the past, and keep taking your medicines as prescribed. If four weeks feels like too much, test one suspect food at a time with the two-week method from the food chapter.

You don't have to do this at all. If your joints feel the same whatever you eat, that's worth knowing too, and you can enjoy your meals without worry.

The strict approach

Some nutrition books, including the a well-known nutrition reference book I drew from, go much further. They advise cutting all of these at once:

  • Milk, dairy products and red meat
  • Caffeine, citrus fruits and paprika
  • Everything containing sugar, and tobacco
  • Most added fat
  • Salt. If you use any at all, keep it to a little Celtic salt, which is unrefined and keeps small amounts of magnesium, potassium and calcium that table salt loses.
  • The nightshade vegetables: peppers, eggplant, tomatoes and white potatoes. The book says they contain solanine, which some people with arthritis are sensitive to, and which may cause muscle pain.

No trial has shown that everyone with arthritis needs to cut all of these, and some people who try it notice no change. Others feel a real difference. If you'd like to find out which group you're in, treat it as a test: follow the strict list for three weeks, then bring back one food group every three days and note your pain in your tracker. Keep anything that makes no difference. If you cut dairy and meat, get calcium from greens, almonds and fortified plant milk, and protein from beans, lentils and tofu.

Poultices, packs and warmth

Poultices are the best known of all the natural remedies, and there is real comfort in tending a sore joint with your own hands. One of them, cayenne, works for a lot of people.

Cayenne

Helps

Start here. Capsaicin, the compound that gives chili its heat, quiets pain signals when used regularly. A pharmacy capsaicin cream is the easiest way to use it, because the strength is measured for you. It is recommended for knee osteoarthritis in particular.

  1. Apply a thin layer to the sore joint three or four times a day.
  2. Expect warmth or stinging for the first one to two weeks. It fades as the nerves settle.
  3. Wash your hands afterwards and keep it away from eyes and broken skin.
  4. Give it two to four weeks before judging it. Relief builds quietly.

Heat and cold

May help

These are simple and they work for many people. Warmth loosens stiffness; give it 15 to 20 minutes with a heat pack, a warm shower or a wheat bag. A cold pack wrapped in a cloth calms a hot, swollen joint. A good rule: warm what is stiff, cool what is swollen. Skip both on skin that feels numb. A hot shower or bath first thing in the morning is one of the simplest ways to loosen morning stiffness. Some people also rub fresh lemon juice into a sore joint; stop if it stings.

Alternating hot and cold water

May help

This is a good first thing to reach for when a joint hurts, and it works best for hands, wrists, feet and ankles.

  1. Fill one bowl with comfortably warm water and one with cold. Warm means bath temperature, about 40°C (104°F), never hot enough to redden your skin.
  2. Soak the joint in the warm bowl for three minutes, then in the cold bowl for 30 seconds.
  3. Repeat three times, about ten to twelve minutes in all.
  4. Dry off and move the joint while it's warm and loose.

Some people say their pain drops by half in those twelve minutes. Research on alternating baths is limited, so take that as their experience and not a promise. Warmth and cold each ease pain for a while, and this is a pleasant way to use both. If you have numbness, poor circulation or diabetes affecting your feet, check with your doctor first.

For hips, knees and shoulders

A bowl won't reach these, so here are two ways. The easy one is the shower: with a handheld shower head, run comfortably warm water over the joint for three minutes, then cool water for 30 seconds, three times. The other is a warm, wrung-out towel laid over a dry towel on the joint, swapped for a cold cloth. Some people wring the towel out of boiling water. Please use hot tap water and test it on your inner wrist first, because boiling water scalds.

Ginger poultice

May help

Many people call this the best poultice for a sore joint. Use it straight after the hot and cold water.

  1. Grate a heaped teaspoon of fresh ginger onto the center of a thin cotton cloth about 20 cm (8 inches) square. A piece of old sheet is ideal.
  2. Fold the sides in to make a flat parcel, and place the single-layer side against your skin.
  3. Cover it with a piece of plastic and bandage it on, snug but not tight.
  4. Start with one joint, for up to an hour in the early evening and never straight before bed. Then remove it and rinse the skin.

Some people wear one for up to three hours, or even overnight. The shorter time is easier on your skin. The warmth you feel is the ginger stimulating your skin. You may hear that burning is inflammation being drawn out. Burning is simply irritated skin, so take the poultice off as soon as it stings. Wearing one three times in a day can leave blisters. If that happens, rest the skin for a week. Eating ginger has the better evidence, and you'll meet it in the next chapter.

Castor oil compress

Yours to try

This is the overnight remedy. Barbara O'Neill wore one on a sore thumb joint every night for six months and says the tenderness went.

  1. Fold soft cotton cloth into about six layers, or use three layers of toweling, large enough to wrap the joint.
  2. Pour a small pool of castor oil into the center and leave it for 15 minutes to soak through. Too much and it will leak onto your bedding.
  3. Wrap it round the joint, cover with plastic and bandage it on overnight.
  4. Fold it away in the morning and reuse it for a month or more, adding half a teaspoon of oil about once a week.

    The warm version, for daytime. Warm the castor oil gently in a small pan, never letting it boil, and soak a piece of cotton flannel in it. Lay it on the sore joint, cover it with plastic a little larger than the cloth, and rest a heating pad set on low on top for 30 minutes to two hours. Never fall asleep with a heating pad on.

It hasn't been tested in trials for arthritis, and one person's experience can't tell us whether the oil, the warmth or simply time did the healing. It's mild on unbroken skin, though, and many people find the warmth and stillness a comfort. You may also hear that it breaks up scar tissue, bone spurs and calcified joints. Nothing supports that, so use it for comfort and expect no more. Keep it on the outside only; swallowed, castor oil is a strong laxative.

Epsom salt bath

Yours to try

A warm bath is one of life's reliable comforts, and it eases stiff joints and tired muscles. There is no good evidence that magnesium passes through the skin, so the relief comes from the warm water itself. Add the salts if you enjoy them.

Warm wax for hands

May help

A paraffin wax bath, found at pharmacies and used by hand therapists, wraps stiff fingers in even heat. It is suggested in guidelines for hand osteoarthritis and can make the first movements of the day much easier.

Comfrey cream

May help

Comfrey cream is an old remedy for an aching joint, and this one has some real support: in a three-week trial of 220 people, comfrey root ointment eased knee osteoarthritis pain far more than a dummy cream, though the study was run by the maker. Buy a ready-made cream, use it on unbroken skin for a few weeks at a time, and never swallow comfrey or use it in pregnancy, because the plant contains compounds that can harm the liver.

Skin safety

Test any poultice on a small patch first. Do not leave a ginger or cayenne poultice on overnight, and do not use heat over a cream that already warms the skin.

  • Never apply heat or cold for more than 20 minutes at a time.
  • Keep a cloth between a cold pack and your skin.
  • If you have diabetes or poor circulation, check with your doctor before using heat or cold on your feet.

Herbs and spices

Many of the oldest arthritis remedies come from plants. Here is what each one offers, what the studies found, and what to watch for.

Turmeric

May help

There is good reason for hope here. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health describes the early evidence for knee osteoarthritis as positive, though higher-quality studies are still needed. A 2025 review that pooled 17 trials came to the same place: turmeric does seem to ease knee osteoarthritis pain, while the quality of the proof is still low. Cooking with turmeric, or stirring it into warm milk, is a pleasant habit; just know it gives far less curcumin than the extracts used in trials.

Turmeric doses, made safe

Some talks suggest taking up to 8,000 mg a day. Osteoarthritis trials have mostly used around 500 to 1,500 mg of curcumin extract a day, so that is many times the tested amount, and higher doses bring more stomach upset and more risk. Go above the dose on the label only after speaking with your doctor. You may also hear that cayenne can stand in for black pepper as an absorption booster. It doesn't. That compound, piperine, comes from black pepper, while cayenne's is capsaicin. Cayenne in your cooking is fine. It just won't boost turmeric.

Cautions for turmeric supplements

  • Standard products are considered likely safe for up to two to three months.
  • "High-absorption" formulations have been linked to liver injury. Stop and see a doctor if you notice dark urine, yellowing skin, nausea, poor appetite or unusual tiredness.
  • Supplements may be unsafe in pregnancy. Turmeric in food is fine.
  • Check with a pharmacist if you take blood thinners or diabetes medication.

Ginger

May help

In food, tea or a supplement, ginger has eased osteoarthritis pain a little. A review of five trials in nearly 600 people found a small but real reduction in pain and disability. Go easy if it gives you heartburn, and mention it to your pharmacist if you take blood thinners.

Boswellia (frankincense)

May help

Boswellia is a resin used for centuries in Ayurvedic medicine. In small trials, extracts standardized to boswellic acids, especially one called AKBA, eased knee osteoarthritis pain and stiffness within a few months. Look for a product that states its boswellic acid content on the label. It can upset the stomach, so take it with food.

Willow bark

May help

Willow bark contains salicin, a natural relative of aspirin, and has been used for pain for thousands of years. Trials in low back pain found it eased pain better than a dummy pill. Results in osteoarthritis are mixed. Skip it if you're allergic to aspirin, take blood thinners or anti-inflammatory tablets, or have kidney or stomach problems.

Devil's claw

May help

A root from southern Africa. Several trials in hip and knee osteoarthritis and in back pain found it eased pain modestly, and in one study it did about as well as a prescription arthritis medicine. It can upset the stomach. Avoid it with stomach ulcers or gallstones, and ask first if you take blood thinners or diabetes medicine.

Cat's claw

May help

A vine from the Amazon rainforest. In one small study of people with rheumatoid arthritis, adding it to their usual medicine meant fewer painful joints, and a small osteoarthritis study also found less knee pain. Because it may act on the immune system, ask your rheumatologist first, and don't use it while pregnant or breastfeeding. If you take prescription blood thinners, check with your doctor before using it, as the reference book advises for its partner herb, feverfew.

Stinging nettle

Yours to try

Nettle leaf has a long history in Germany for sore joints. In one small trial, people pressed fresh nettle leaves onto a painful thumb or finger joint daily for a week and had less pain than with a look-alike leaf. Taken by mouth, the evidence is thin. Ask first if you take blood thinners, water pills, or diabetes or blood pressure medicine.

Feverfew

Not shown to help joints

The reference book pairs feverfew with ginger for pain and soreness. Feverfew is better known for migraine. In a trial in rheumatoid arthritis it did no better than a dummy pill. Don't use it while pregnant, and ask first if you take blood thinners. Ginger, earlier in this chapter, has the better evidence.

Olive leaf extract

Yours to try

The reference book suggests it for arthritis caused by infection, because compounds in the leaf can kill germs in the lab. There are no good arthritis trials. A joint infection needs a doctor and antibiotics the same day, so treat this as a pleasant extra, not a treatment. Olive oil in your food is the part of the olive with strong support.

Alfalfa

Avoid if you have lupus or an immune condition

Alfalfa is rich in minerals, and the reference book says it may help arthritis. There's no good evidence that it does. Alfalfa seeds and sprouts contain a compound that has triggered lupus-like flares, so if you have lupus, rheumatoid arthritis or another immune condition, leave it out.

Yucca

Yours to try

A desert plant whose root is rich in natural soap-like compounds. Only a few very small studies exist, with hints of less pain and stiffness. It can upset the stomach, and long use may affect how you absorb some vitamins.

Celery seed

Yours to try

A traditional remedy for gout and joint pain, sold as tablets or tea. There are no good trials. Don't use it in pregnancy, and ask first if you have kidney problems or take blood thinners or water pills.

Noni

Yours to try

Noni, from a Polynesian fruit, has been used for pain for over 2,000 years. There are no good arthritis trials. The juice is very high in potassium, so avoid it with kidney disease, and stop it if you notice signs of liver trouble such as yellowing skin or dark urine.

Others the reference names

The same reference book also lists brigham tea, buchu leaves, burdock root, corn silk, parsley tea, a Chinese formula called Duhuo Jisheng Wan, and an extract of phellodendron bark sold as Nexrutine. Each has very little research behind it for arthritis. If you try one, try it alone for a few weeks, and keep it only if you feel a difference. Phellodendron should not be used in pregnancy or by children.

Thunder god vine

Please avoid

This herb appears in some arthritis blends. It can cause serious side effects, and health authorities warn that its risks may outweigh its benefits.

Supplements: what to take and how much

The supplement aisle makes a great many promises. This chapter will save you money and worry by telling you plainly which ones have earned their place, and how much to take.

Fish oil

May help in rheumatoid arthritis

A review of 22 trials found that fish oil modestly reduces pain in rheumatoid arthritis, and it may ease morning stiffness. The parts that do the work are the omega-3 fats EPA and DHA, and the label tells you how much of each is in a capsule. It has not been shown to help osteoarthritis. In a nine-month study of people with rheumatoid arthritis, 39 in every 100 who took cod liver oil each day were able to cut their anti-inflammatory painkillers by more than a third, compared with 10 in every 100 on a dummy capsule. Another small study found fish oil worked better when taken together with olive oil. Eating oily fish twice a week is the simplest way to get these oils. If you'd prefer capsules, ask your doctor first, especially if you take blood thinners. And if you eat only plants, as Barbara O'Neill does, the plant oils below and a spoonful of ground flaxseed each day are the ones to look at.

Evening primrose and borage oil

May help in rheumatoid arthritis

These plant seed oils contain a fat called GLA, which the body uses to make substances that calm inflammation. A review of the trials found they eased pain and tender joints a little in rheumatoid arthritis, and one study found fish oil and evening primrose oil together reduced the number of tender joints. Choose a borage oil labeled free of PA (pyrrolizidine alkaloids). Ask your doctor first if you take blood thinners or have epilepsy.

Bromelain (from pineapple)

Yours to try

Bromelain is an enzyme found in pineapple stems. In one small study of adults with mild knee pain, 400 mg a day eased pain more than 200 mg a day. The study had no dummy group, so treat it as a promising sign, not proof. Fresh pineapple is a pleasant way to eat a little of it. Skip the capsules if you take blood thinners or are allergic to pineapple.

MSM

Yours to try

MSM is a sulfur compound sold for joints, often mixed with glucosamine. A few small trials found a modest easing of knee pain. The evidence is thin, so give it two months and keep it only if you can feel a difference.

Glucosamine and chondroitin

Free to use

These are the best-selling joint supplements in the world, which is why this may surprise you. In a two-year study of 222 people with hip osteoarthritis, 1,500 mg of glucosamine a day did no better than a dummy pill, for pain or for the joint itself. A review of 20 chondroitin trials found no real benefit for hips or knees. Osteoarthritis guidelines don't recommend glucosamine. If you want to try it, give it two months. If nothing has changed by then, it isn't going to. They are safe for most people, so if you feel they help, you're free to carry on. If you've been buying them for years without feeling a difference, you're just as free to stop.

Vitamin D

Only if you're low

Vitamin D matters for bones, and it's worth correcting if a blood test shows you're short. Taking extra has not been shown to ease arthritis pain.

Iron supplements

Only if a blood test shows you're low

The a well-known nutrition reference book I drew from advises against iron supplements, and against multivitamins that contain iron, because iron is suspected of adding to pain, swelling and joint damage. It suggests getting iron from food instead: blackstrap molasses, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower, fish, lima beans and peas. One thing to check first: many people with rheumatoid arthritis are low in iron, and that can be part of the tiredness. Ask for a blood test before you take or stop iron, and follow what it shows.

Shark cartilage

Not shown to help

Sold to ease pain and repair joints. The reference book suggests starting at 1 gram for every 15 pounds of body weight each day, split into three doses, then dropping to 1 gram per 40 pounds once you feel relief. That is a lot of capsules, and there are no good trials showing it helps arthritis. It can upset the stomach and is high in calcium.

DMSO

May help a little, on the skin

DMSO is a liquid made from wood processing, rubbed on sore joints to ease pain and swelling. A review of small trials found it may reduce osteoarthritis pain a little. Use only pure DMSO from a pharmacy or health store, never the industrial kind from a hardware store, because it carries whatever is on your skin into your body. It often leaves a garlic-like taste and smell.

Bee venom

Only with a trained professional

Honeybee venom, given by injection or with live stings, is used for arthritis pain in some parts of the world. Small trials, mostly from Asia, suggest it may ease pain, but they vary in quality. It can trigger a severe allergic reaction, so never try it at home, and never at all if you're allergic to bee stings.

Hair analysis

Not reliable

Some practitioners test a hair sample to look for toxic metals such as lead, which have been found at higher levels in some people with arthritis. When researchers sent identical hair samples to different labs, the results disagreed widely. A blood test from your doctor is the reliable way to check for lead.

Movement, your strongest medicine

If you take only one thing from this book, let it be this chapter. Movement is the most powerful natural remedy there is, and here the popular advice and the medical guidelines agree completely.

Movement

Helps

The American College of Rheumatology and the Arthritis Foundation strongly recommend exercise for osteoarthritis, and consistent exercise is the strongest recommendation in the guideline for rheumatoid arthritis too. It feeds cartilage, strengthens the muscles that protect your joints, lifts your mood and deepens your sleep.

You may be worried that exercise will wear your joints out faster. It's a very common fear, and the good news is that sensible movement does not speed up arthritis. Stillness is what makes joints stiffer, weaker and more painful.

Four kinds of movement, and what each gives you

  • Walking, cycling or swimming. Builds stamina and eases stiffness. Work toward 30 minutes on most days, in pieces as short as five or ten minutes.
  • Strength work. Strong thighs take load off knees; strong hips steady your walk. Twice a week is enough to feel a difference.
  • Tai chi or yoga. Slow, flowing movement that builds balance and calm. Tai chi is strongly recommended for knee and hip osteoarthritis, and yoga is suggested for knees.
  • Water exercise. Warm water carries your weight, so sore joints can move freely. Many people who dread exercise on land love it in a pool.
Three that don't jar the joints

A mini trampoline, an exercise bike and swimming all move you without jarring the joints. The bike and the pool are excellent choices with good research behind them. Swimming is the first choice when joints are very sore, because the water carries your weight, and a kickboard or the pool edge is all you need if you can't swim. The bike suits anyone unsteady on their feet. A mini trampoline works too if you keep your feet on the mat and simply lift your heels, sometimes called the health bounce. Choose one with a support bar, because balance comes first. You may hear that bouncing clears waste through the lymph system. Any movement helps blood and lymph to flow, so you don't need a trampoline for that.

How to know you're doing it right

  • Mild soreness that settles within a day is normal and safe. It means your body is adapting.
  • If you hurt more than usual for over two hours afterwards, do a little less next time. Less, not nothing.
  • Sharp, sudden or lasting pain means stop that movement and ask a physical therapist.
  • On good days, resist the urge to do everything. Steady beats heroic.

A physical therapist can build a program around the joints that hurt, which matters most if you have been inactive or have had a joint replaced.

If moving hurts today, start smaller than feels worthwhile. Five minutes is not nothing. It's how the easier mornings begin.

A lighter load, deeper sleep, a calmer mind

Remedy books rarely mention these three, yet each one turns pain down and helps the other remedies work.

Weight

Helps

Losing excess weight is strongly recommended for knee and hip osteoarthritis, and the reason is encouraging. A study of 142 adults with knee osteoarthritis found that each pound lost took about four pounds of pressure off the knee with every step. Over a single mile, that adds up to thousands of pounds your knees no longer have to carry. In one careful eighteen-month trial, people with knee osteoarthritis who paired a lighter diet with exercise cut their pain by about half, far more than diet or exercise managed alone. Weight loss also lowers the risk of gout flares.

You do not need to reach an "ideal" weight to feel this. Losing even a little brings relief, and the eating pattern from the food chapter is the easiest way to get there. No crash diets. They don't last, and you deserve better.

One couple's story

A couple in their late sixties, both overweight and sore, had moved into a care home. They changed how they ate, gave up their nightly wine, drank more water and exercised every morning, and over six months they lost around 45 pounds. Their knees and feet felt the difference, their blood tests improved, and their doctor took them off their anti-inflammatories. A year later they left the care home, bought a camper and set off around Australia. It is one couple's story and not a promise. Notice what did the work, though: a lighter load, daily movement, and a doctor who adjusted their medicines as they improved. That is exactly the path this book lays out for you.

Sleep

May help

Poor sleep lowers your pain threshold, so everything hurts more the next day, and pain then disturbs the following night. You can break that circle from the sleep side.

  • Keep a regular bedtime and waking time, even at weekends.
  • Take a warm shower or bath before bed to loosen stiff joints.
  • Keep the room dark and cool, with a little fresh air if you like it, and ease off caffeine after lunch.
  • Tuck a pillow between your knees if you sleep on your side, or under them if you sleep on your back.
  • If pain wakes you most nights, tell your doctor. It is treatable.

A calmer mind

May help

Stress and worry tighten muscles and sensitize nerves. Calming practices do the opposite. Studies in rheumatoid arthritis found that mindfulness and similar approaches eased pain and stiffness as a useful addition to standard care, and talking therapy for pain is suggested in osteoarthritis guidelines.

A two-minute calm

Sit comfortably and rest your hands in your lap. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four and out through your mouth for a count of six. Repeat ten times. A longer out-breath tells your nervous system it is safe to soften. Try it now, before the next chapter.

Setting down resentment

May help

You read earlier how Barbara O'Neill's mother carried anger about a move she never wanted, and how the family came to believe it fed her illness. Feelings do not cause arthritis, and you did not bring yours on yourself. What is true is that long-held anger keeps the body tense and pain turned up. Talking it through, forgiving where you can, praying if you pray, or simply writing it down can lighten what you carry.

Being kind to your joints all day

Relief isn't only found in remedies. It's also found in how you carry a bag, open a jar and plan your afternoon. Occupational therapists teach these habits, and they can spare your joints a great deal of strain.

Share the load

  • Use your largest, strongest joints for the job. Push a door with your shoulder or forearm, and carry bags on your forearm or in a backpack.
  • Use two hands where you used to use one, and slide heavy things across the counter where you'd once have lifted them.
  • Hold things with a loose, open grip. Thick-handled pens, knives and tools make this easy.

Pace yourself

  • Break big jobs into pieces and rest briefly between them, before pain tells you to.
  • Change position every 20 to 30 minutes. Joints dislike being held still.
  • Spread demanding tasks across the week so no single day carries them all.

Helpful tools are a sign of wisdom

  • A jar opener, a kettle tipper and lever faucets protect sore hands.
  • Cushioned, supportive shoes ease knees and hips with every step. High heels do the opposite: in one study, even moderate heels raised the strain on the inner knee by about a quarter. A flat or low heel is kinder.
  • A walking stick, held in the hand opposite your sore leg, takes real load off a painful knee or hip. Guidelines strongly recommend one when it helps you move more.
  • Thumb splints and knee braces are recommended for some people with osteoarthritis. A therapist can fit one properly.

A movement guide for sore joints

These ten movements are easy on sore joints and need no equipment beyond a sturdy chair. Move slowly, breathe, and stop short of sharp pain. If you've had a joint replaced or a recent injury, ask your physical therapist which ones suit you.

  1. Seated march. Sit tall and lift one knee, then the other, for one minute. This warms the hips and knees.
  2. Sit to stand. From a chair, stand up and sit back down slowly. Do 5 to 10. Use your hands at first if you need them.
  3. Heel raises. Hold the chair back and rise onto your toes, then lower. Do 10. This steadies ankles and calves.
  4. Knee straightening. Seated, straighten one leg, hold for five seconds, lower. Do 10 each side. Strong thighs protect knees.
  5. Side leg lift. Hold the chair back and lift one leg out to the side, toes forward, then lower. Do 8 each side. Strong hips steady every step.
  6. Wall push-ups. Hands on a wall, bend your elbows and press back. Do 8 to 10. Easier on wrists than the floor.
  7. Shoulder circles. Roll both shoulders slowly backward 10 times, then forward 10 times.
  8. Hand opening. Make a soft fist, then spread your fingers wide. Do 10. Best done after warming your hands in water.
  9. Thumb touches. Touch your thumb to each fingertip in turn, making a round "O" each time. Go through twice on each hand.
  10. Ankle circles. Lift one foot and circle the ankle 10 times each way. Repeat on the other side.

Begin with one round. When that feels easy, do two. Twice a week is plenty to start, and every round counts.

When to see a doctor

Looking after yourself includes knowing when to bring someone in. If you don't have a diagnosis yet, start there, and please don't wait on any of the signs below.

Same-day care

  • One joint suddenly hot, red and very painful, especially with fever. This can be a joint infection.
  • A joint that cannot bear weight after a fall or injury.

An appointment within weeks

  • Swelling in several joints, or morning stiffness lasting more than an hour.
  • Joint pain with rashes, fatigue, weight loss or eye problems.
  • Pain that wakes you at night or keeps getting worse.

Treatments that work alongside natural care

  • Osteoarthritis: anti-inflammatory gels are strongly recommended for knees and are easy on the rest of the body. Tablets, steroid injections and, when pain and function call for it, joint replacement are there if you need them.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: disease-modifying drugs such as methotrexate. Starting early is what prevents lasting joint damage.
  • Gout: urate-lowering medication such as allopurinol if flares recur, usually aiming for a blood uric acid level below 6 mg/dL.

Using pain relievers safely

  • Anti-inflammatory tablets such as ibuprofen and naproxen help many people, but taken regularly they can cause stomach ulcers or bleeding, raise blood pressure and strain the kidneys. Take them with food, and ask your doctor about stomach protection, especially if you're over 65 or have had stomach bleeding before. They also make the body hold on to water, so go easy on salt, Celtic salt included, while you take them.
  • Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is easier on the stomach, but too much of it, or taking it with alcohol, can harm the liver. Stay within the dose on the label.
  • Change, add or stop a pain reliever only after speaking with your doctor.

Conditions that can look like arthritis

Some illnesses cause joint pain before anything else shows. Lyme disease can follow a tick bite, sometimes with a spreading rash and flu-like aches, and it is treated with antibiotics. Lupus and inflammatory bowel disease, such as ulcerative colitis, can also start with sore joints. If your joint pain came with any of these, tell your doctor.

Tell your doctor and pharmacist about every herb and supplement you use.

Your weekly action plan

The most studied idea in arthritis self-care isn't a food or a stretch. It's believing you can manage your arthritis, and then proving it to yourself one small week at a time.

Researchers at Stanford call it self-efficacy. In their long-running studies, raising that belief improved people's health even more than the changes in their habits did. It's the heart of The Arthritis Helpbook by Kate Lorig and James Fries, and it's simple to use.

How to write one

  1. Pick one small, specific thing. Instead of "exercise more," try "walk ten minutes after lunch on Monday, Wednesday and Friday."
  2. Ask yourself how sure you are that you'll do it, from 0 to 10.
  3. If the answer is below 7, make the plan smaller until it's 7 or more.
  4. At the end of the week, look at how it went and write next week's plan.

Your pain toolbox

  • Warmth before activity; a wrapped cold pack for a hot, swollen joint, up to 20 minutes.
  • Slow breathing, distraction and mindfulness, which change how the brain handles pain signals.
  • Massage from a trained therapist.
  • Your medicines as prescribed, including anti-inflammatory gels for hands and knees.

When tiredness is the problem

  • Split big jobs into short blocks with rests, and save the hard ones for your best time of day.
  • Keep a regular bedtime, and ease pain before you go to bed.
  • Regular movement builds energy over a few weeks, even though rest feels better in the moment.
  • In rheumatoid or other inflammatory arthritis, deep tiredness can mean the disease is active. Tell your doctor.

Questions people often ask

How soon will I feel a difference?

Capsaicin cream usually needs two to four weeks. Exercise often eases stiffness within a few weeks, with more benefit over two to three months. Be patient with yourself.

Can I do this instead of my medication?

Only after speaking with your doctor. Everything here is meant to work with your treatment. If you'd like to take less medication, your doctor can lower it safely as you improve, and a good tracker makes it easier.

Exercise hurts. Should I stop?

Mild discomfort that settles within a day is safe and expected. Sharp pain, or pain that lingers, means do less next time, not nothing.

Will exercise wear out my joints?

No. Sensible movement feeds cartilage and strengthens the muscles that protect it. Stillness is what joints fear.

Is cracking or creaking a bad sign?

Usually not. Joints that click without pain or swelling are simply noisy.

Does the weather really affect arthritis?

Many people feel worse in cold, damp weather, and you're not imagining it. Keep warm, keep moving, and know that it passes when the weather does.

Are the remedies safe with other health conditions?

Mostly, but check turmeric and ginger supplements with a pharmacist if you take blood thinners or diabetes medication, and avoid capsaicin on broken skin.

Can I heal completely?

Some kinds can. Joint infections and Lyme arthritis are cured with the right antibiotics, and gout flares can stop for good once uric acid is kept low. Most people with rheumatoid arthritis who are treated early now reach remission, which means no active disease. With osteoarthritis, pain and stiffness often improve so much that life opens up again. "Can arthritis be reversed?" in Part one has the details.

Am I too old to start?

You are not. People in their seventies, eighties and nineties gain strength and ease from exercise. The best day to begin is the one you're in.

I've tried everything and I'm tired. Is it worth it?

Your tiredness makes sense. You don't need to try everything again. Pick the single smallest step in week 1 and begin there. That is enough for today.

Other voices worth hearing

Barbara O'Neill isn't the only person with a following for natural arthritis care. If you spend time in the library, you've probably come across a few of these names too. Here is what each one teaches, and what's worth taking from it.

Most of them agree with doctors on the basics: move every day, eat more plants, keep your weight down, sleep well and lower stress. They part ways when they suggest medicine can be dropped. On those points the evidence is mostly personal stories.

Clint Paddison and the Paddison Program

Only with your doctor

Paddison built his program after his own rheumatoid arthritis. It is a mostly plant-based diet that drops meat, dairy, oil and processed food to settle the gut and calm inflammation. It starts with a two-day cleanse, then about two weeks of very plain "baseline" eating, and then foods come back one at a time. Exercise and stress care are part of it.

Arthritis New Zealand shared the story of one member who followed it while staying on methotrexate. She reported being about 90% pain-free after six months, with no active inflammation found, and said the plain baseline weeks were the hardest part. The charity's own view is blunt: it is "not an evidence-based diet recommended for any condition." If yours is rheumatoid arthritis, your medicine is what prevents lasting joint damage, so talk to your doctor before starting a program like this and keep taking what you've been prescribed.

Dr. Susan Blum, Healing Arthritis

Yours to try

Dr. Blum is a physician who has lived with an autoimmune condition herself. Her book looks for the root causes of inflammation in the gut, in food and in stress, and readers like how practical it is. It hasn't been tested as a program in large trials. The habits it asks for, though (more plants, less processed food, better sleep and less stress), overlap with what this book already recommends.

Dr. D.C. Jarvis, Arthritis and Folk Medicine (1960)

Free to use

A Vermont country doctor who collected the remedies of local farm families. His book made apple cider vinegar famous as an arthritis drink, and people still pass it around today. No clinical trials show that it helps joints. If you enjoy it, dilute it well and drink it through a straw, because the acid is hard on teeth. Skip it if you have reflux.

Dr. Collin Dong, New Hope for the Arthritic (1975)

Free to use

Dr. Dong cut out dairy, fruit, red meat and chocolate, believing food chemicals and allergies set arthritis off. At the time the Arthritis Foundation said diet had nothing to do with it. The truth sits in between: diet matters a great deal in gout and for weight, and some people do have personal trigger foods. Part two shows you a safe way to find yours.

What they all share

Look past the differences and the same habits turn up in every one of these books: caring for the whole person, food as medicine, moving every day, rest and less stress, and taking charge of your own care. Those are also the habits research supports most, so that's where your energy goes first.

Extra reading

Three hundred years of remedies

Long before supplements and phone apps, people with "rheumatism" and gout were trying lemons, celery, milk diets and hot baths. Some of what they found still holds. Plenty doesn't.

For about 300 years, writers blamed rheumatism and gout on eating too much, on too much meat, and on "poisons" building up in the joints. They prescribed plain food, fruit, vegetables and patience. A few of their books are now free to read online, and they show how people thought.

A milk diet for gout (1700s)

History

A European physician named Dolæus wrote that a strict, simple milk diet could cure gout. His English translator, William Stephens, said the real value of the book was its case histories. Nobody uses milk-only diets now, though low-fat milk is linked with fewer gout flares, so he wasn't entirely wrong.

The lemon cure (1908)

History

In Food Remedies, Florence Daniel recommended lemon juice in hot water, night and morning. The full "lemon cure" climbed from one lemon a day to twelve, and Daniel herself thought that went too far. She called celery "almost a specific" for rheumatism and gout, and praised potatoes steamed in their skins, with the cooking water used to bathe swollen joints.

Her explanation was that fruit acids "purify the blood." That part hasn't survived. A plate full of vegetables has.

Earthy deposits (early 1900s)

History

Henry Lindlahr's Nature Cure taught that glue-like, "earthy" deposits build up in rheumatic joints, and that natural living dissolves them. You can hear the same idea a century later when Barbara O'Neill talks about breaking up calcification. No study has shown that food, poultices or oils dissolve bone spurs. Gout crystals can dissolve, but it's medicine that lowers uric acid that does it.

Hope for your arthritis (1948)

History

This booklet told readers that nine in ten patients could expect some benefit, and that more than 85% of deformities could be prevented. The hope, it said, came from earlier diagnosis, better nutrition and patients who stuck with their care, rather than from any miracle drug. It warned against "overnight cures." Close to 80 years later, that advice still stands.

What held up, and what didn't

  • Held up: gout really is linked to rich food, red meat and alcohol.
  • Held up: fruit and vegetables really are linked to lower risk.
  • Held up: early diagnosis really does prevent deformity.
  • Didn't: "blood poisons," cures from lemons alone, and milk-only diets.
