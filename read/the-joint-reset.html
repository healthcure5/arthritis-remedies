And everyone has advice. Cut this food. Drink that juice. Try the cream your neighbor swears by. Hurting is tiring enough. Not knowing who to believe wears you out even more.

I wrote this book so that the day you're having now doesn't have to be the day you're having three months from now. It hands you the steps that turn stiff, careful days into easier ones, and it stays with you until those steps feel like your own.

Relief today

If you're hurting right now, start here. These are the quickest comforts in the book. None takes longer than half an hour, and each one points to its full page if you want to know more.

  1. Warm what's stiff. Stand in a hot shower, or rest a heat pack or wheat bag on the joint for 15 to 20 minutes. For sore hands, soak them in a bowl of comfortably warm water. There's more in "Poultices, packs and warmth."
  2. Cool what's swollen. If a joint feels hot and puffy, wrap a cold pack in a thin cloth and hold it there for 15 minutes.
  3. Switch between hot and cold water for hands, wrists, feet and ankles. Three minutes in warm water, 30 seconds in cold, three times over, finishing on cold.
  4. Rub in cayenne cream. Capsaicin cream from the pharmacy, on your sorest joint, three or four times a day. It stings at first, and the sting fades over a week or two. Wash your hands well afterward.
  5. Move a little. A minute of marching in your chair, ten ankle circles, ten slow openings of each hand. Moving feeds the joint and helps turn pain down. The full set is in "A movement guide for sore joints."
  6. Breathe out slowly. Take ten slow breaths, letting each breath out last longer than the breath in. It settles a nervous system that's been turning the pain up.

Call a doctor today if one joint turns suddenly hot, red and very painful, especially with a fever, or if a joint can't take your weight after a fall.

Part one · Understand

What's happening inside your joints

You don't need a medical degree to understand your joints. You just need a clear picture. Once you have it, you'll feel more in charge of your body than you have in a long while.

A joint is where two bones meet. The ends of those bones are capped with cartilage, a smooth, springy tissue that lets them glide past each other. The whole joint sits inside a thin lining that makes a slippery fluid, a bit like oil in a door hinge. The muscles and tendons around it hold it steady and carry much of the load.

Why movement is medicine

Cartilage has no blood supply of its own. It's fed by that joint fluid, and the fluid only moves in and out of the cartilage when the joint moves. So every bend and stretch is a meal for your cartilage, and long spells of rest leave it hungry. Barbara O'Neill, whose talks shaped much of this book, puts it simply: movement brings blood, and blood brings what a joint needs. That's why the joints you use often, without forcing them, tend to serve you longest.

What goes wrong

Arthritis disturbs that system. The cartilage may thin and roughen. The lining may swell and become inflamed. In gout, crystals form inside the joint. However it starts, you end up with the same family of problems you already know too well: pain, stiffness, swelling, and a joint that tires quickly.

Pain isn't the same as damage

If you keep one idea from this chapter, keep this one. Pain is a signal your nervous system makes, and lots of things turn it up or down. Poor sleep turns it up. So do stress, worry, cold weather, a low mood, and sitting still too long. Plenty of joints that look worn on an X-ray feel fine, and plenty that look healthy still hurt. So a bad day doesn't mean your joints are crumbling. It means your system is touchy today, and you have more ways to calm it than you might think.

The three levers you hold. Everything that helps in this book does at least one of three things. It takes load off the joint, it calms inflammation, or it settles an oversensitive nervous system. Movement, sleep and food do all three at once, which is why you'll keep running into them.

Know your arthritis

Each kind of arthritis needs something different, so knowing which one you have is worth more than any single remedy. Popular talks often lump them together under one cause, like "acidity" or "toxins." They're really different conditions, and understanding yours puts you back in the driver's seat.

You're far from alone. About 53 million American adults, roughly one in five, have been told by a doctor that they have arthritis. Osteoarthritis is by far the most common, with more than 32 million. Gout affects about 9 million Americans, most of them men. Rheumatoid arthritis affects around 1.3 to 1.5 million, and women are two to three times more likely to get it than men.

The big three

Osteoarthritis. Cartilage and the tissue around it slowly wear and try to repair themselves, most often in the knees, hips, hands and spine. It usually feels like stiffness that eases within half an hour of getting up, pain that builds the more you use the joint, and joints that creak or look knobbly. What helps most is exercise, losing weight if you carry extra, and pain relief, with joint replacement for some.

Rheumatoid arthritis. Here the immune system attacks the joint lining, often in both hands or both feet at the same time. Mornings bring stiffness that lasts more than an hour. The joints feel warm and swollen on both sides of the body, and many people feel deeply tired. Prescription medicine that calms the immune system, started early, is what controls it.

Gout. Crystals of uric acid form inside a joint, very often the big toe. One joint turns hot, red and painfully tender, sometimes overnight, then settles over a few days. It's controlled with medicine that lowers uric acid, along with changes to food and alcohol.

Other kinds you may hear about

  • Psoriatic arthritis brings joint swelling to people who have the skin condition psoriasis. About one in three people with psoriasis develop it, and it can affect the spine as well.
  • Ankylosing spondylitis is inflammation in the spine. It feels like a stiff, aching lower back that's worst in the morning and eases once you get moving. It usually starts before 45.
  • Lupus is an immune condition that can bring joint pain along with a rash, deep tiredness and trouble in other organs. It's far more common in women.
  • Fibromyalgia causes aches all over, poor sleep and tiredness. It can feel like arthritis, but it doesn't damage the joints.
  • Bursitis and tendinitis cause a sore spot next to a joint, like the shoulder, elbow or hip, often after overuse. They usually settle within days or weeks.
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome brings tingling or numbness in the thumb and first fingers, often worse at night, from pressure on a nerve in the wrist.
  • A joint infection makes one joint hot, red and swollen, often with a fever. This needs a doctor the same day.

Psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis and lupus are driven by the immune system, just like rheumatoid arthritis, and a rheumatologist looks after them. If you're not sure which kind you have, getting a diagnosis is the best first step, because the right treatment depends on it.

Two more things are worth knowing about rheumatoid arthritis. Women who breastfed, especially for a year or more in total, turn out to be less likely to develop it. And people with rheumatoid arthritis often run low on folate, protein and zinc, partly because of their medicines. A well-filled plate and the occasional blood test are worth it.

If yours is rheumatoid arthritis, please hold on to this. The medicine is what protects your joints from lasting damage, and the first months matter most. Let the remedies in this book comfort you alongside it.

Starting early, and letting your doctor fine-tune the medicine at each visit until the joints go quiet, is why so many people now reach what doctors call remission. A Finnish register found that about 69% of people with rheumatoid arthritis were in remission in a recent year. That's a world away from a generation ago, and it's within reach for many people who start now.

Does where you live matter? No. The countries where the most people with rheumatoid arthritis reach remission don't share a climate or a special diet. What they share is early diagnosis and steady treatment. The part that matters most comes with you wherever you go: start early, keep moving, and work with your doctor.

Can arthritis be reversed?

For some kinds, yes. For others, the pain and stiffness can turn around even when the joint itself doesn't change much. Here's what's possible for each.

Kinds that can be cleared

  • A joint infection, treated early with the right antibiotics, is usually cleared completely.
  • Lyme arthritis clears up with antibiotics in most people.
  • Reactive arthritis, which can follow a gut or urinary infection, often goes away on its own within a few months to a year.

Kinds that can go quiet for years

  • Gout. Keeping uric acid in the blood below 6 mg/dL slowly dissolves the crystals in the joint. Over months to years, the flares can stop altogether.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis. When treatment starts early, most people reach remission, meaning no active disease. A smaller group, guided by their doctor, later keep that remission on less medicine or none at all.

Osteoarthritis: turning the pain around

Studies haven't shown worn cartilage growing back. But how your knee or hip feels can change a great deal. In an 18-month study of older adults with knee osteoarthritis who carried extra weight, the ones who lost about a tenth of their weight through food and exercise cut their knee pain roughly in half. Stronger muscles, a lighter load and daily movement are what make that kind of change possible.

The nutrition reference book I drew on for parts of this edition goes further. It says some forms of arthritis can be reversed, even cured, with the right food and habits, easing inflammation and pain, stopping the breakdown and renewing the joint. The first part, easing pain and inflammation through food, weight and movement, has good research behind it. Renewing worn cartilage hasn't been proven yet, so hold that part as a hope.

Who she is

Barbara O'Neill is an Australian natural health teacher. For many years she and her husband, Michael, ran the Misty Mountain Health Retreat at Nulla Creek in New South Wales. Why her story stays with me

Barbara was in her early twenties when she watched her mother begin to slip away. It started soon after a house move her mother never wanted, sometime in her forties, with a stiffness in her hands that wouldn't lift. Then her hands stopped working. Then her legs. The name for it was rheumatoid arthritis. She was given long-term steroids, anti-inflammatories and gold injections, and still she went downhill, year after year, until she needed a wheelchair. She died at 51. Barbara's father believed to the end of his life that the anger his wife never let go of about that move had helped the illness along. In her last weeks, a family friend who trusted natural remedies stepped in with fresh juices, a plate full of plants, water, sunshine and slow, patient massage, and for about six weeks the pain eased its grip.

What stays with me isn't the illness. It's the daughter standing beside that bed. If you've ever watched someone you love hurt and felt there was nothing in your hands to give them, you know exactly how she felt. One thing has changed since then, and it changes the ending for you. Barbara's mother was treated more than fifty years ago, before the medicines we have now. Today's rheumatoid arthritis drugs do more than dull the ache. They can slow or stop the damage that once took people's hands and legs, which is why her mother's story is far rarer now. And while a heavy heart can keep pain turned up, no feeling causes rheumatoid arthritis. Her mother didn't bring it on herself, and neither did you.

Listen closely and much of what she says is plain good sense. Drink water. Sleep well. Eat mostly plants. Go easy on sugar. Keep a healthy weight. Move every day. Nine daily habits

Underneath every remedy in this book sit nine daily habits. Barbara O'Neill calls them the laws of health, and they're a good frame whether or not you follow the rest of her teaching. Their first letters spell two easy words: SUSTAIN ME.

  • S, Sunshine. Get a little daylight every day. It lifts your mood and tops up your vitamin D. Enjoy it, and protect fair skin.
  • U, Use of water. Drink around eight glasses a day. It's good for your whole body, and it helps keep gout flares away.
  • S, Sleep. Keep a regular bedtime and get enough rest. Good sleep turns pain down.
  • T, Trust. Hold on to faith, gratitude and hope. A calm, hopeful mind feels less pain.
  • A, Abstain. Stop smoking, and go easy on alcohol and sugar. Smoking makes rheumatoid arthritis worse, and alcohol sets off gout.
  • I, Inhale. Breathe slowly through your nose, and let fresh air into the room. It calms your body.
  • N, Nutrition. Fill most of your plate with plants.
  • M, Moderation. Keep portions sensible. A healthy weight takes load off your knees.
  • E, Exercise. Move every day. Nothing in this book does more for your joints.

A simple way to find what works

Barbara uses a routine that's worth borrowing. Look back over your history, notice what your symptoms are telling you, change one habit, and watch how your body responds. Her test is one question: "Does it bring relief?" It's a sound way to try any mild remedy, and it's exactly what the tracker in Part four is for. Just keep your doctor in the loop, because some causes can't be found by feel alone.

Three ideas from the talks to take to your doctor first

  • "Drugs never cure, they only mask symptoms." For rheumatoid arthritis, today's medicines do more than that. They protect joints from permanent damage. Some talks suggest easing off medicine as your habits change. Do that only after speaking with your doctor, who can lower a dose safely as you improve.
  • "Autoimmune disease isn't real." It is. Rheumatoid arthritis shows up in blood tests and scans. It can go quiet for long stretches, which doctors call remission, and that's most often reached with treatment.
  • "Castor oil breaks up tumors, bone spurs, scar tissue and calcified joints." Nothing shows this. Any new lump needs a doctor, not a pack.

Eight myths, and what's true

You'll hear these from friends, online, and sometimes in the doctor's waiting room. Each one can keep you stuck, so here's a plain answer to each.

"Arthritis is just wear and tear from getting old."

Not quite. The National Institutes of Health says osteoarthritis isn't simply wearing out, and children get arthritis too. Age raises the risk, but it doesn't decide your future.

"Rest your joints. Exercise makes it worse."

The opposite. Movement lowers pain and makes everyday tasks easier. The right exercise protects your joints. Sitting still is what stiffens them.

"Nothing can be done."

Even a booklet from 1948 said nine in ten people could be helped. You have far more to work with today: movement, weight loss, good medicines and the habits in this book.

"Glucosamine rebuilds cartilage."

The biggest trials found it did very little, and rheumatology guidelines advise against it.

"Diet doesn't matter."

It does. Losing extra weight clearly helps, food matters a great deal in gout, and eating mostly plants looks promising in small trials.

"Acidic blood dissolves the cartilage in your joints."

Some nutrition books say this, including the reference book I drew from. In fact your body holds your blood's acid level in a tight range, between 7.35 and 7.45, whatever you eat. If it ever moved far enough to harm cartilage, you'd be seriously ill in hospital, not sore at home. Eating lots of fruit and vegetables is still a great idea, for other reasons you'll find in the food chapter.

"Natural means safe."

Not always. Some supplements harm the liver, clash with medicines, or secretly contain prescription drugs. Tell your pharmacist about everything you take.

"A natural program can replace my rheumatoid arthritis medicine."

Arthritis New Zealand, the NHS and the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health all warn that putting off rheumatoid arthritis medicine risks permanent damage. Natural care belongs beside it, not in its place.

The remedies at a glance

Here's the whole picture on one page, so you can see where to put your energy first. Four remedies make the biggest difference for most people. Seven may help. Six are comforts you're welcome to try. Five are popular rules you can relax about. And two are only for after a talk with your doctor.

Helps: start here

  • Moving every day. It keeps your joints fed. Guidelines for osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis both recommend it strongly.
  • Losing extra weight. It takes load off your joints, and it's strongly recommended for knee and hip osteoarthritis.
  • Cayenne cream on the skin. Capsaicin cream eases pain and is recommended for knee osteoarthritis.
  • Tai chi. Slow strength and balance work, strongly recommended for knee and hip osteoarthritis.

May help

  • Eating mostly whole plant foods. Good for your weight and your health overall. A Mediterranean-style way of eating is suggested for rheumatoid arthritis.
  • Turmeric. Early trials for knee osteoarthritis are encouraging.
  • Ginger, eaten or as a poultice. Eating it gives a small benefit. The poultice soothes mostly through warmth.
  • Hot and cold packs. Recommended for short-term relief of stiffness and swelling.
  • Switching between hot and cold water. Warmth and cold each ease pain for a while. The back-and-forth routine is less studied, but it's safe with comfortably warm water.
  • Omega-3s from plants and algae. Omega-3 fats bring modest relief from pain and morning stiffness in rheumatoid arthritis. Flax, chia, walnuts and algae oil give you them without fish.
  • Comfrey cream. A three-week trial found it eased knee osteoarthritis pain. Use it on unbroken skin only, and never swallow it.

Yours to try

  • Castor oil packs. Not proven, but the warmth and the rest can feel lovely.
  • Carrot, celery and apple juice. A pleasant drink with no proven effect on joints. Whole vegetables do more.
  • Epsom salt baths. The warm water is what soothes. The salt is up to you.
  • Lemon water in the morning. Refreshing and harmless. Rinse with plain water afterward to protect your teeth.
  • A big breakfast and a light supper. A few small studies suggest eating more earlier in the day makes weight loss easier.
  • Breathing through your nose. Calming, even though it doesn't change the oxygen in your joints.

Relax about these

  • Cutting out nightshades like tomatoes, peppers and potatoes. Research hasn't found a link, so keep the foods you love. The two-week test in this book will tell you if they bother you.
  • "Alkalizing" your body. Food doesn't change your blood's acid level. The vegetables help for other reasons.
  • Celtic salt in water. Barbara O'Neill likes it for its trace minerals. For your joints it's the water doing the work, and check with your doctor first if you have high blood pressure.
  • Glucosamine. Safe for most people, but the biggest trials found little or no benefit.
  • Cutting wheat or cooked greens. Nothing shows these worsen arthritis, so keep them. Dairy is a different story, which you'll find in "Milk, yogurt and cheese" in Part two.

Only with your doctor

  • Turmeric at 8,000 mg a day. That's many times what the trials used. The dose on the label is the one that was tested.
  • Cutting back your medicine. Your doctor can lower a dose safely as you improve, which matters most in inflammatory arthritis.

Part two · Soothe

Food and drink

Food is one of the easiest tools you have, and the way it helps is simple. It eases the load on your joints, calms inflammation and looks after the rest of you. You don't need to chase acid or toxins to feel the difference.

Eating to calm inflammation

May help

This is where the popular advice gets it right. Fill your plate with vegetables, fruit, beans, nuts and whole grains, and go easy on sugar and processed food. Mainstream doctors say the same thing. A Mediterranean-style way of eating, built on olive oil, vegetables, beans and whole grains, has strong research behind it, and the American College of Rheumatology suggests it for people with rheumatoid arthritis. A fully plant-based plate goes one step further.

This isn't a passing fad. Back in 1991, a study in the Lancet followed people with rheumatoid arthritis who switched to plant-based eating. Their swollen joints and morning stiffness eased, and the benefit was still there a year later. The change came slowly, and it was real. Good food works alongside your treatment, not instead of it, and the researchers who ran that study said so themselves.

Omega-3 fats, without the fish

May help

Two omega-3 fats, called EPA and DHA, help your body make substances that calm inflammation. In rheumatoid arthritis, they bring a modest easing of joint pain and morning stiffness. Most people think of fish when they hear omega-3, but fish don't make these fats. They build them up by eating algae, and you can go straight to the source.

Why leave the fish out. Oily fish like sardines, anchovies, mackerel and herring are high in purines, which your body turns into uric acid, the cause of gout flares. Some fish also carry mercury and other pollutants from the water they live in. Plants give you the good fats without either problem.

The plant way. Barbara O'Neill eats only plants, and so did the guests at her retreat. For protein, she points to beans, lentils, nuts and seeds, which she calls the cleanest-burning protein: lentils, chickpeas, beans, tofu and hummus. Her oils come from plants too. Olive oil goes into cooking and salads, and the rest of her fat comes from avocado, nuts and seeds. She steers people away from cheap refined seed oils.

Each day, add a spoonful of ground flaxseed, chia or hemp seeds, or a handful of walnuts. They carry a plant omega-3 called ALA. Your body turns only a small part of ALA into EPA and DHA, so if you'd like those two directly, algae oil has them. Take it only after speaking with your doctor.

Nightshades

You can keep them

You may have heard that people with arthritis should give up potatoes, tomatoes, peppers and eggplant, because of plant chemicals called lectins. Research hasn't found that link. The Arthritis Foundation calls it a myth, and these foods are good for you.

Still, people differ, and your own body has the final say. If you suspect a food, leave it out for two weeks, bring it back, and see what happens. Peeling and deseeding tomatoes and peppers does no harm if you want to try it.

One exception is worth knowing. Some people with gout do find that tomatoes set off a flare, and one study linked them with higher uric acid. If you have gout, the two-week test is especially worth doing. And sweet potatoes aren't nightshades at all.

Alkaline eating

Keep the food, drop the worry

If you've felt better eating this way, I believe you. Give the credit to all those vegetables. The idea behind it is that food makes the fluid around your cells and joints acidic. Your body guards that fluid as carefully as it guards your blood, and no study has shown food changing the acidity inside a joint. So enjoy the meals, and let go of any fear of "acidic" foods.

The 80/20 plate

A popular rule says to fill 70 to 80% of your plate with "alkaline" foods and keep everything else to 20 or 30%. Set the chemistry aside, and what's left is simply a plate full of plants, which is good advice.

  • The 80%: leafy greens and other vegetables, fruit, lemon, millet, quinoa, buckwheat, spelt, kamut, einkorn, almonds, Brazil nuts, lima beans, lentils, soy and seeds.
  • The 20%: modern wheat, other grains, other beans and other nuts, with sugar, caffeine and alcohol kept lowest of all. These lists put meat and aged cheese here too, though this book suggests swapping them for plants.
  • One correction: walnuts, oats and chickpeas usually end up on the "acid" side of these lists. They're some of the best foods there are for your joints and your heart. Eat them freely.
  • Cooked greens are welcome. You may be told to eat spinach and chard only raw, because cooking makes them acidic. It doesn't. Cooked greens are just as good for you, and much easier to eat in big helpings.

Carrot, celery and apple juice

Yours to try

A well-known joint juice is about 80% carrot, 10% celery and 10% apple, taken once or twice a day. It's a pleasant drink and an easy way to get vegetables in. It hasn't been shown to ease joints, clear uric acid or change your body's acidity, though, and juice has more sugar and less fiber than whole vegetables. One small glass is plenty. If you have diabetes, count it as you would any juice.

Lemon water

Yours to try

The juice of a lemon in a glass of water each morning is refreshing and harmless, though it won't make you alkaline. Rinse with plain water afterward to protect your teeth.

Water, and Celtic salt

The water is what matters

Drinking enough through the day, around eight glasses, is an easy habit, and it helps keep gout flares away.

If you like a little salt with your water, Barbara O'Neill uses Celtic salt, the gray, slightly damp sea salt from the coast of France, and no other. She chooses it because it's unrefined. It keeps small amounts of magnesium, potassium and calcium that are stripped out of white table salt, and it has nothing added to stop it clumping. She means a single crystal on the tongue before a glass of water. It has nearly as much sodium as table salt, so check with your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, heart trouble or kidney trouble. And if Celtic is the only salt in your kitchen, get your iodine from a little seaweed, like nori or a sprinkle of dulse flakes, because iodized table salt is where most people get theirs.

The sipping trick. If you skip water because you're worried about running to the bathroom, add just one glass a day and sip it a few mouthfuls at a time. If your doctor has set a fluid limit for your heart or kidneys, stick to that.

Sugar and processed food

Less is better

Refined sugar gets a lot of the blame, and on the practical side the blame is fair. Sweet drinks, pastries and heavily processed meals make your weight harder to manage, and they tend to leave people feeling more inflamed and worn out. You don't have to give them up forever. Swap out one a day, and you'll start to feel lighter.

Tea and coffee

You can keep them

Tea sometimes takes the blame for arthritis. Nothing good shows that a normal amount of tea or coffee causes arthritis or makes it worse, and both count toward your water for the day. If caffeine keeps you up at night, stop after lunch.

Wheat and bread

You can keep them

Modern wheat often gets blamed for inflammation, with older grains like spelt, kamut and einkorn suggested in its place. Those are good grains, and wholegrain wheat of any kind is good for you too. Nothing shows wheat makes arthritis worse unless you have celiac disease or a diagnosed sensitivity. If you suspect one, ask your doctor for a celiac test before you cut wheat out, because the test only works while you're still eating it.

Milk, yogurt and cheese

Swap them for plants

Aged cheese often turns up on "acid" lists, but acid isn't the real reason to cut back. Cheese is the biggest source of saturated fat in the American diet, and whole milk and butter add more. That weighs on your heart and your waistline. The nutrition reference book I drew on advises cutting dairy for arthritis, and some people find dairy is one of their own trigger foods. The two-week test in the next chapter shows you how to check.

The swaps are easy. Pour oat, almond or soy milk into your oatmeal and tea, and try a plant yogurt with fruit. For calcium, eat leafy greens like kale, collards and bok choy, plus almonds, tahini, calcium-set tofu and fortified plant milk.

Meat and eggs

Swap them for plants

Cutting out meat helps, though not because of acid. Red meat and organ meats are full of purines, which your body breaks down into uric acid, the cause of gout flares. Meat carries saturated fat, and processed meats like bacon, ham and sausages add plenty of refined salt. In large studies, people who eat the most red and processed meat tend to show more signs of inflammation in their blood. Eggs add saturated fat and cholesterol, and nothing your joints can't get from plants.

Everything meat gives you, plants can give you too.

  • Protein: lentils, beans, chickpeas, tofu, tempeh, edamame, quinoa and hemp seeds.
  • Iron: beans, lentils, dark leafy greens, pumpkin seeds and blackstrap molasses. Eat them with something rich in vitamin C, like a squeeze of lemon or a few strawberries, to help your body take the iron in.
  • Zinc: pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, cashews and oats.
  • Vitamin B12: the one nutrient plants can't give you. Take a B12 supplement, or use fortified plant milk or nutritional yeast, and ask your doctor to check your level once a year.

Barbara O'Neill's swap is beans, lentils, nuts and seeds. Start by having lentils or beans instead of meat a few nights a week, then keep going. Season them well with garlic, onion, cumin, turmeric and fresh herbs, and you won't miss the meat.

A big breakfast and a light supper

Yours to try

The old saying goes: breakfast like a king, lunch like a queen, supper like a pauper. A few small studies suggest that eating more of your food earlier in the day makes weight loss a little easier, and many people sleep better after a light evening meal. If you take medicine with food at night, keep doing that.

Four more foods worth adding

  • Garlic, onions, leeks and asparagus. They're rich in sulfur, which your body uses to build and repair cartilage and connective tissue. In a study of more than 1,000 women twins, the ones who ate the most from the onion family showed fewer signs of hip osteoarthritis.
  • Fiber, every day. Ground flaxseed, oat bran, rice bran, beans and whole grains. In a large US study, people who ate the most fiber were less likely to develop painful knee osteoarthritis. A spoonful of ground flaxseed a day also brings you the plant omega-3.
  • Tart cherries. The reference book suggests about 20 a day, and frozen ones are fine. A small trial of tart cherry juice in knee osteoarthritis found it eased pain and stiffness a little. There's more on cherries and gout just below.
  • Fresh pineapple, often. It contains bromelain, the enzyme you'll read about in the supplements chapter. Canning and freezing destroy it, so buy it fresh.

If yours is gout

Food matters more in gout than in any other kind of arthritis, so here's what protects you. The American College of Rheumatology suggests cutting back on alcohol, beer and spirits most of all. It also suggests limiting purine-rich foods like organ meats, red meat and shellfish, and avoiding drinks sweetened with high-fructose corn syrup. Losing extra weight helps too.

Cherries deserve a place in your fruit bowl. In a study of more than 600 people with gout, those who ate cherries over a two-day stretch had about a third fewer flares in the days that followed. A serving was ten to twelve cherries, and frozen ones count. It isn't proven, but it's a pleasant thing to try. Vitamin C tablets aren't recommended. And if flares keep coming back, medicine that lowers uric acid is what stops them for good.

Finding your own trigger foods

Some people notice that one particular food seems to set their joints off. Others never find a link at all. A short, careful test is the only way to know which group you're in.

Even the NHS, which says there's no strong evidence that diet changes rheumatoid arthritis, notes that some people find certain foods make them worse, and that leaving those foods out for a few weeks can help you spot them.

Plant-based eating for rheumatoid arthritis

May help

In 2022, Dr. Neal Barnard's team ran a trial with 44 adults who had rheumatoid arthritis. After 16 weeks on a low-fat vegan diet, the average number of swollen joints dropped from 7 to about 3, and people lost around 14 pounds without counting calories. It was a small study, and the group behind it campaigns for plant-based eating, so call it promising rather than proven.

The four-week test

This comes from Dr. Barnard's book Foods That Fight Pain.

  1. For four weeks, eat foods that rarely cause trouble. Oats, rice, quinoa, millet, buckwheat and lentils. Cooked greens like broccoli, kale, spinach and asparagus. Cooked carrots, squash and zucchini. And cooked or dried fruit that isn't citrus, like pears, apricots, blueberries and plums.
  2. Leave out the common triggers completely: dairy, chocolate, eggs, citrus, meat, wheat, nuts and peanuts, tomatoes, onions, corn, apples and bananas. Skip alcohol and caffeine for the four weeks too.
  3. If you feel better, bring one food back every two days, in a good-sized helping, and watch for a flare. Write it down in your tracker.
  4. If a food sets off a flare, take it back out and wait at least two weeks before you try it again. Many people find more than one.

Before you start. This is a big change. Check with your doctor first if you have diabetes, are underweight, take blood thinners or have struggled with eating in the past, and keep taking your medicines as prescribed. If four weeks feels like too much, test one suspect food at a time with the two-week method from the food chapter.

And you don't have to do this at all. If your joints feel the same whatever you eat, that's useful to know too, and you can enjoy your meals without worrying.

The strict approach

Some nutrition books, including the reference book I drew on, go much further. They advise cutting all of these at once:

  • Milk, dairy products and red meat
  • Caffeine, citrus fruit and paprika
  • Anything with sugar in it, and tobacco
  • Most added fat
  • Salt. If you use any at all, keep it to a little Celtic salt, which is unrefined and keeps small amounts of magnesium, potassium and calcium that table salt loses.
  • The nightshade vegetables: peppers, eggplant, tomatoes and white potatoes. The book says they contain solanine, which some people with arthritis are sensitive to and which may cause muscle pain.

No trial has shown that everyone with arthritis needs to cut all of these, and some people who try it notice no change. Others feel a real difference. If you want to know which group you're in, treat it as a test. Follow the strict list for three weeks, then bring back one food group every three days and note your pain in your tracker. Keep anything that makes no difference. While you're off dairy and meat, get your calcium from greens, almonds and fortified plant milk, your protein from beans, lentils and tofu, and your vitamin B12 from fortified foods or a supplement.

Poultices, packs and warmth

Poultices are the best known of all the natural remedies, and there's something comforting about tending a sore joint with your own hands. One of them, cayenne, works for a lot of people.

Cayenne

Helps

Start here. Capsaicin, the compound that gives chili its heat, quiets pain signals when you use it regularly. A pharmacy capsaicin cream is the easiest way to use it, because the strength is measured for you. It's recommended for knee osteoarthritis in particular.

  1. Rub a thin layer into the sore joint three or four times a day.
  2. Expect warmth or stinging for the first week or two. It fades as the nerves settle.
  3. Wash your hands afterward, and keep it away from your eyes and any broken skin.
  4. Give it two to four weeks before you decide. The relief builds slowly.

Heat and cold

May help

Simple, and they work for many people. Warmth loosens stiffness, so give it 15 to 20 minutes with a heat pack, a warm shower or a wheat bag. A cold pack wrapped in a cloth calms a joint that's hot and swollen. The rule of thumb: warm what's stiff, cool what's swollen. Skip both on skin that feels numb. A hot shower or bath first thing in the morning is one of the easiest ways to shake off morning stiffness. Some people also rub fresh lemon juice into a sore joint. Stop if it stings.

Switching between hot and cold water

May help

When a joint hurts, this is a good first thing to reach for. It works best on hands, wrists, feet and ankles.

  1. Fill one bowl with comfortably warm water and another with cold. Warm means bath temperature, about 40°C (104°F), never so hot it turns your skin red.
  2. Soak the joint in the warm bowl for three minutes, then in the cold bowl for 30 seconds.
  3. Do that three times, about ten to twelve minutes in all.
  4. Dry off, and move the joint while it's warm and loose.

Some people say their pain drops by half in those twelve minutes. Research on switching baths is thin, so take that as their experience rather than a promise. Warmth and cold each ease pain for a while, and this is a pleasant way to use both. If you have numbness, poor circulation or diabetes that affects your feet, check with your doctor first.

For hips, knees and shoulders. A bowl won't reach these, so try one of two ways. The easy one is the shower. With a handheld shower head, run comfortably warm water over the joint for three minutes, then cool water for 30 seconds, three times over. The other way is a warm, wrung-out towel laid over a dry towel on the joint, then swapped for a cold cloth. Some people wring the towel out of boiling water. Please use hot tap water instead, and test it on the inside of your wrist first, because boiling water scalds.

Ginger poultice

May help

Many people call this the best poultice for a sore joint. Use it straight after the hot and cold water.

  1. Grate a heaped teaspoon of fresh ginger onto the middle of a thin cotton cloth about 20 cm (8 inches) square. A piece of old sheet works well.
  2. Fold the sides in to make a flat parcel, and put the single-layer side against your skin.
  3. Cover it with a piece of plastic and bandage it on, snug but not tight.
  4. Start with one joint, for up to an hour in the early evening, never right before bed. Then take it off and rinse your skin.

Some people wear one for up to three hours, or even overnight, but the shorter time is kinder to your skin. The warmth you feel is the ginger stirring up your skin. You may hear that a burning feeling means inflammation is being drawn out. It just means your skin is irritated, so take the poultice off as soon as it stings. Wearing one three times in a day can leave blisters, and if that happens, give your skin a week off. Eating ginger has better evidence behind it, and you'll find it in the next chapter.

Castor oil pack

Yours to try

This one works overnight. Barbara O'Neill wore one on a sore thumb joint every night for six months and says the tenderness went away.

  1. Fold soft cotton cloth into about six layers, or use three layers of towel, big enough to wrap the joint.
  2. Pour a small pool of castor oil into the middle and leave it 15 minutes to soak through. Use too much and it'll leak onto your sheets.
  3. Wrap it around the joint, cover it with plastic, and bandage it on for the night.
  4. Fold it away in the morning. You can reuse it for a month or more, adding half a teaspoon of oil about once a week.

The warm daytime version. Warm the castor oil in a small pan, never letting it boil, and soak a piece of cotton flannel in it. Lay it on the sore joint, cover it with plastic a little bigger than the cloth, and rest a heating pad set on low on top for 30 minutes to two hours. Never fall asleep with a heating pad on.

Castor oil packs haven't been tested in arthritis trials, and one person's experience can't tell us whether the oil, the warmth or simply time did the work. It's mild on unbroken skin, though, and many people find the warmth and stillness a real comfort. You may also hear that it breaks up scar tissue, bone spurs and calcified joints. Nothing backs that up, so use it for comfort. Keep it on the outside only, because swallowed, castor oil is a strong laxative.

Epsom salt bath

Yours to try

A warm bath is one of life's reliable comforts, and it eases stiff joints and tired muscles. Nothing good shows that magnesium soaks in through the skin, so the relief comes from the warm water. Add the salts if you enjoy them.

Warm wax for hands

May help

A paraffin wax bath, sold at pharmacies and used by hand therapists, wraps stiff fingers in even heat. Guidelines suggest it for hand osteoarthritis, and it can make the first movements of the day much easier.

Comfrey cream

May help

Comfrey is an old remedy for an aching joint, and this one has some real support. In a three-week trial of 220 people, comfrey root ointment eased knee osteoarthritis pain far more than a dummy cream, though the company that makes it ran the study. Buy a ready-made cream and use it on unbroken skin for a few weeks at a time. Never swallow comfrey or use it during pregnancy, because the plant contains compounds that can harm the liver.

Looking after your skin

Test any poultice on a small patch of skin first. Don't leave a ginger or cayenne poultice on overnight, and don't put heat over a cream that already warms the skin.

  • Never use heat or cold for more than 20 minutes at a time.
  • Keep a cloth between a cold pack and your skin.
  • If you have diabetes or poor circulation, check with your doctor before using heat or cold on your feet.

Herbs and spices

Many of the oldest arthritis remedies come from plants. Here's what each one offers, what the studies found, and what to watch out for.

Turmeric

May help

There's good reason to be hopeful about this one. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health calls the early evidence for knee osteoarthritis positive, though it wants better studies. A 2025 review that pooled 17 trials landed in the same place: turmeric does seem to ease knee osteoarthritis pain, but the proof is still weak. Cooking with turmeric, or stirring it into warm oat or almond milk, is a lovely habit. Just know it gives you far less curcumin than the extracts used in the trials.

About the dose. Some talks suggest up to 8,000 mg a day. The osteoarthritis trials mostly used around 500 to 1,500 mg of curcumin extract a day, so 8,000 mg is many times what was tested, and higher doses bring more stomach upset and more risk. Go above the dose on the label only after speaking with your doctor. You may also hear that cayenne can stand in for black pepper to help your body absorb turmeric. It can't. The compound that helps, piperine, comes from black pepper. Cayenne's compound is capsaicin. Cayenne in your cooking is fine. It just won't help the turmeric.

Turmeric supplements: what to watch for

  • Standard products are thought to be safe for up to two or three months.
  • "High-absorption" products have been linked to liver injury. Stop and see a doctor if you notice dark urine, yellowing skin, nausea, a poor appetite or unusual tiredness.
  • Supplements may not be safe in pregnancy. Turmeric in food is fine.
  • Check with a pharmacist if you take blood thinners or diabetes medicine.

Ginger

May help

Whether in food, tea or a supplement, ginger has eased osteoarthritis pain a little. A review of five trials with nearly 600 people found a small but real drop in pain and disability. Go easy if it gives you heartburn, and mention it to your pharmacist if you take blood thinners.

Boswellia (frankincense)

May help

Boswellia is a tree resin used for centuries in Ayurvedic medicine. In small trials, extracts with a set amount of boswellic acids, especially one called AKBA, eased knee osteoarthritis pain and stiffness within a few months. Look for a product that lists its boswellic acid content on the label. It can upset your stomach, so take it with food.

Willow bark

May help

Willow bark contains salicin, a natural cousin of aspirin, and people have used it for pain for thousands of years. Trials in low back pain found it worked better than a dummy pill. Results in osteoarthritis are mixed. Skip it if you're allergic to aspirin, take blood thinners or anti-inflammatory tablets, or have kidney or stomach problems.

Devil's claw

May help

A root from southern Africa. Several trials in hip and knee osteoarthritis and in back pain found it eased pain a little, and in one study it did about as well as a prescription arthritis medicine. It can upset the stomach. Avoid it if you have stomach ulcers or gallstones, and ask first if you take blood thinners or diabetes medicine.

Cat's claw

May help

A vine from the Amazon rainforest. In one small study of people with rheumatoid arthritis, adding it to their usual medicine meant fewer painful joints, and a small osteoarthritis study also found less knee pain. Because it may affect the immune system, ask your rheumatologist first, and don't use it while pregnant or breastfeeding. If you take prescription blood thinners, check with your doctor before using it.

Stinging nettle

Yours to try

Nettle leaf has a long history in Germany as a remedy for sore joints. In one small trial, people pressed fresh nettle leaves onto a painful thumb or finger joint every day for a week and had less pain than people using a look-alike leaf. As a tea or tablet, the evidence is thin. Ask first if you take blood thinners, water pills, or medicine for diabetes or blood pressure.

Feverfew

Not shown to help joints

The reference book pairs feverfew with ginger for pain and soreness. Feverfew is better known for migraine, and in a rheumatoid arthritis trial it did no better than a dummy pill. Don't use it while pregnant, and ask first if you take blood thinners. Ginger, above, has the better evidence.

Olive leaf extract

Yours to try

The reference book suggests it for arthritis caused by infection, because compounds in the leaf kill germs in the lab. There are no good arthritis trials. A joint infection needs a doctor and antibiotics the same day, so think of this as a pleasant extra, not a treatment. Olive oil in your food is the part of the olive with strong support.

Alfalfa

Avoid if you have lupus or another immune condition

Alfalfa is rich in minerals, and the reference book says it may help arthritis. Nothing good shows that it does. Alfalfa seeds and sprouts contain a compound that has set off lupus-like flares, so if you have lupus, rheumatoid arthritis or another immune condition, leave it out.

Yucca

Yours to try

A desert plant whose root is full of natural soap-like compounds. Only a few very small studies exist, with hints of less pain and stiffness. It can upset the stomach, and long use may affect how well you absorb some vitamins.

Celery seed

Yours to try

An old remedy for gout and joint pain, sold as tablets or tea. There are no good trials. Don't use it in pregnancy, and ask first if you have kidney problems or take blood thinners or water pills.

Noni

Yours to try

Noni juice, from a Polynesian fruit, has been used for pain for more than 2,000 years. There are no good arthritis trials. It's very high in potassium, so avoid it if you have kidney disease, and stop if you notice signs of liver trouble like yellowing skin or dark urine.

Others the reference book names

The same reference book also lists brigham tea, buchu leaves, burdock root, corn silk, parsley tea, a Chinese formula called Duhuo Jisheng Wan, and an extract of phellodendron bark sold as Nexrutine. Each has very little research behind it for arthritis. If you try one, try it on its own for a few weeks, and keep it only if you feel a difference. Phellodendron shouldn't be used in pregnancy or by children.

Thunder god vine

Please avoid

This herb turns up in some arthritis blends. It can cause serious side effects, and health authorities warn its risks may outweigh its benefits.

Supplements: what to take and how much

The supplement aisle makes a lot of promises. This chapter will save you money and worry by telling you plainly which ones have earned a place, and how much to take.

One thing first. Your kitchen comes before the pill bottle. Most of what helps your joints is on your plate, in a warm pack, or in a short walk. If you do want to try a supplement, try one at a time, and only after speaking with your doctor.

Omega-3 oils from algae

May help in rheumatoid arthritis

A review of 22 trials found that omega-3 oils ease pain a little in rheumatoid arthritis, and they may help morning stiffness. The fats doing the work are called EPA and DHA. In one nine-month study of people with rheumatoid arthritis, 39 out of every 100 who took omega-3 oil each day were able to cut their anti-inflammatory painkillers by more than a third. Only 10 out of 100 on a dummy capsule managed the same. These oils haven't been shown to help osteoarthritis.

Most of those trials used fish oil, but fish only carry these fats because they eat algae. Algae oil gives you the same EPA and DHA straight from the source, with no fishy aftertaste, no ocean pollutants and no animal products. The label tells you how much of each is in a capsule. Food still comes first, as Barbara O'Neill teaches: a spoonful of ground flaxseed every day, with chia seeds and walnuts. If you'd like capsules as well, take them only after speaking with your doctor, especially if you take blood thinners.

Evening primrose and borage oil

May help in rheumatoid arthritis

These seed oils contain a fat called GLA, which your body uses to make substances that calm inflammation. A review of the trials found they eased pain and tender joints a little in rheumatoid arthritis, and one study found evening primrose oil worked better alongside an omega-3 oil. If you choose borage oil, look for one labeled free of PA (pyrrolizidine alkaloids). Ask your doctor first if you take blood thinners or have epilepsy.

Bromelain, from pineapple

Yours to try

Bromelain is an enzyme found in pineapple stems. In one small study of adults with mild knee pain, 400 mg a day eased pain more than 200 mg a day. There was no dummy group, though, so call it a promising sign rather than proof. Fresh pineapple is a pleasant way to get a little of it. Skip the capsules if you take blood thinners or are allergic to pineapple.

MSM

Yours to try

MSM is a sulfur compound sold for joints, often mixed with glucosamine. A few small trials found it eased knee pain a little. The evidence is thin, so give it two months and keep it only if you can feel a difference.

Glucosamine and chondroitin

Relax about these

These are the best-selling joint supplements in the world, so this may surprise you. In a two-year study of 222 people with hip osteoarthritis, 1,500 mg of glucosamine a day did no better than a dummy pill, either for pain or for the joint itself. A review of 20 chondroitin trials found no real benefit for hips or knees, and osteoarthritis guidelines don't recommend glucosamine. If you want to try it, give it two months. If nothing has changed by then, it won't. They're safe for most people, so if you feel they help, carry on. If you've been buying them for years and never felt a difference, you're just as free to stop.

Vitamin D

Only if you're low

Vitamin D matters for your bones, and it's worth putting right if a blood test shows you're short. Taking extra hasn't been shown to ease arthritis pain.

Iron

Only if a blood test shows you're low

The nutrition reference book I drew on advises against iron supplements, and against multivitamins with iron in them, because iron is suspected of adding to pain, swelling and joint damage. It suggests getting iron from food instead: blackstrap molasses, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower, lentils, lima beans and peas. Check one thing first, though. Many people with rheumatoid arthritis run low on iron, and that can be part of why they feel so tired. Ask for a blood test before you start or stop iron, and go by what it shows.

Shark cartilage

Not shown to help

It's sold to ease pain and repair joints. The reference book suggests starting at 1 gram for every 15 pounds of body weight a day, split into three doses. That's a lot of capsules, and there are no good trials showing it helps arthritis. It can upset the stomach and is high in calcium. It also comes from an animal, and your joints won't miss it.

DMSO

May help a little, on the skin

DMSO is a liquid made from wood processing, rubbed on sore joints to ease pain and swelling. A review of small trials found it may ease osteoarthritis pain a little. Use only pure DMSO from a pharmacy or health store, never the industrial kind from a hardware store, because it carries whatever is on your skin into your body. It often leaves a garlicky taste and smell.

Bee venom

Only with a trained professional

Honeybee venom, given by injection or with live stings, is used for arthritis pain in some parts of the world. Small trials, mostly from Asia, suggest it may ease pain, but their quality varies. It can cause a severe allergic reaction, so never try it at home, and never at all if you're allergic to bee stings.

Hair analysis

Not reliable

Some practitioners test a lock of hair for toxic metals like lead, which has been found at higher levels in some people with arthritis. When researchers sent identical hair samples to different labs, the results disagreed wildly. A blood test from your doctor is the reliable way to check for lead.

Movement, your strongest medicine

If you only take one thing from this book, take this chapter. Nothing natural does more for your joints than moving them, and on this point the popular advice and the medical guidelines agree completely.

Movement

Helps

The American College of Rheumatology and the Arthritis Foundation strongly recommend exercise for osteoarthritis, and steady exercise is the strongest recommendation in the rheumatoid arthritis guideline too. It feeds your cartilage, strengthens the muscles that protect your joints, lifts your mood and helps you sleep more deeply.

Maybe you're worried exercise will wear your joints out faster. Lots of people are. Here's the good news: sensible movement doesn't speed up arthritis. Stillness is what makes joints stiffer, weaker and sorer.

Four kinds of movement

  • Walking, cycling or swimming build stamina and ease stiffness. Work up to 30 minutes on most days, in pieces as short as five or ten minutes.
  • Strength work pays off fast. Strong thighs take load off your knees, and strong hips steady your walk. Twice a week is enough to feel a difference.
  • Tai chi or yoga means slow, flowing movement that builds balance and calm. Tai chi is strongly recommended for knee and hip osteoarthritis, and yoga is suggested for knees.
  • Water exercise lets warm water carry your weight, so sore joints can move freely. Plenty of people who dread exercise on land love it in a pool.

Three ways to move without jarring your joints. An exercise bike, a pool and a mini trampoline all keep you moving without pounding your joints. The bike and the pool have good research behind them. Swimming is the first choice when your joints are very sore, because the water carries your weight, and if you can't swim, a kickboard or the pool edge is all you need. The bike suits anyone who's unsteady on their feet. A mini trampoline works too if you keep your feet on the mat and just lift your heels, sometimes called the health bounce. Choose one with a support bar, because balance comes first. You may hear that bouncing clears waste through your lymph system. Any movement gets blood and lymph flowing, so you don't need a trampoline for that.

How to tell you're doing it right

  • Mild soreness that settles within a day is normal and safe. It means your body is adjusting.
  • If you hurt more than usual for over two hours afterward, do a little less next time. Less, not nothing.
  • Sharp, sudden or lasting pain means stop that movement and ask a physical therapist.
  • On a good day, resist the urge to do everything. Steady beats heroic.

A physical therapist can build a program around the joints that hurt, which matters most if you've been inactive for a while or had a joint replaced.

If moving hurts today, start smaller than seems worth bothering with. Five minutes counts. That's how easier mornings begin.

A lighter load, deeper sleep, a calmer mind

Remedy books rarely talk about these three. Yet each one turns pain down and helps everything else in this book work better.

Weight

Helps

Losing extra weight is strongly recommended for knee and hip osteoarthritis, and the reason is encouraging. A study of 142 adults with knee osteoarthritis found that every pound lost took about four pounds of pressure off the knee with each step. Over a single mile, that adds up to thousands of pounds your knees no longer have to carry. In one careful eighteen-month trial, people with knee osteoarthritis who paired lighter eating with exercise cut their pain by about half, far more than food or exercise managed on their own. Losing weight also lowers the risk of gout flares.

You don't have to reach some "ideal" weight to feel it. Losing even a little brings relief, and the way of eating in the food chapter is the easiest way to get there. No crash diets. They don't last, and you deserve better.

One couple's story. A couple in their late sixties, both carrying extra weight and both sore, had moved into a care home. But look at what did the work: a lighter load, moving every day, and a doctor who adjusted their medicines as they improved. That's the same path this book lays out for you.

Sleep

May help

Poor sleep lowers your pain threshold, so everything hurts more the next day, and then the pain disturbs the following night. You can break that circle from the sleep side.

  • Keep a regular bedtime and wake-up time, weekends included.
  • Take a warm shower or bath before bed to loosen stiff joints.
  • Keep the room dark and cool, with a little fresh air if you like it, and cut back on caffeine after lunch.
  • Tuck a pillow between your knees if you sleep on your side, or under them if you sleep on your back.
  • If pain wakes you most nights, tell your doctor. Something can be done about it.

A calmer mind

May help

Stress and worry tighten your muscles and make your nerves more sensitive. Calming practices do the opposite. Studies in rheumatoid arthritis found that mindfulness and similar approaches eased pain and stiffness when added to usual care, and talking therapy for pain is suggested in the osteoarthritis guidelines.

A two-minute calm. Sit comfortably and rest your hands in your lap. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four, and out through your mouth for a count of six. Do that ten times. A longer breath out tells your nervous system it's safe to soften. Try it now, before you turn the page.

May help

Being kind to your joints all day

Relief doesn't only come from remedies. It also comes from how you carry a bag, open a jar and plan your afternoon. Occupational therapists teach these habits, and they can spare your joints a lot of strain.

Share the load

  • Let your biggest, strongest joints do the work. Push a door open with your shoulder or forearm, and carry bags on your forearm or in a backpack.
  • Use two hands where you used to use one, and slide heavy things along the counter instead of lifting them.
  • Hold things with a loose, open grip. Thick-handled pens, knives and tools make that easy.

Pace yourself

  • Break big jobs into pieces, and rest for a moment between them, before pain tells you to.
  • Change position every 20 to 30 minutes. Joints don't like being held still.

A movement guide for sore joints

These ten moves are easy on sore joints, and all you need is a sturdy chair. Go slowly, keep breathing, and stop before anything turns into sharp pain. If you've had a joint replaced or a recent injury, ask your physical therapist which ones suit you.

  1. Seated march. Sit tall and lift one knee, then the other, for one minute. It warms up your hips and knees.
  2. Sit to stand. Stand up from a chair and sit back down slowly, 5 to 10 times. Use your hands at first if you need to.
  3. Heel raises. Hold the back of the chair, rise up onto your toes, then lower yourself down. Do 10. They steady your ankles and calves.
  4. Knee straightening. Sitting down, straighten one leg, hold it for five seconds, then lower it. Do 10 on each side. Strong thighs protect your knees.
  5. Side leg lift. Hold the back of the chair and lift one leg out to the side, toes pointing forward, then lower it. Do 8 on each side. Strong hips steady every step you take.
  6. Wall push-ups. Put your hands flat on a wall, bend your elbows, and push back. Do 8 to 10. They're easier on your wrists than the floor.
  7. Shoulder circles. Roll both shoulders slowly backward 10 times, then forward 10 times.
  8. Hand opening. Make a soft fist, then spread your fingers wide. Do 10. This works best after warming your hands in water.
  9. Thumb touches. Touch your thumb to each fingertip in turn, making a round "O" each time. Go through twice on each hand.
  10. Ankle circles. Lift one foot and circle the ankle 10 times each way, then switch feet.

Start with one round. When that feels easy, do two. Twice a week is plenty to begin with, and every round counts.

Your joint-friendly kitchen

Good food steadies your weight, lifts your energy and makes every other step easier. Here's a simple shopping list and eight recipes that ask very little of sore hands.

A shopping list to start with

  • Vegetables: leafy greens, broccoli, carrots, tomatoes, peppers, onions
  • Fruit: berries, cherries, oranges, apples
  • Protein: lentils, chickpeas and black beans for protein and fiber, plus tofu, tempeh, hemp seeds and quinoa
  • Whole grains: oats, brown rice, wholegrain bread
  • Good fats: extra virgin olive oil, walnuts, almonds, chia seeds and ground flaxseed, which carry plant omega-3s
  • Milk: oat, almond or soy milk, ideally fortified with calcium, vitamin D and B12
  • Flavor: turmeric, fresh ginger, garlic, black pepper, lemon

Eight easy recipes

  1. Warm turmeric milk. Heat a cup of oat, almond or soy milk with half a teaspoon of turmeric, a pinch of black pepper and a little honey. A soothing way to end the day.
  2. Fresh ginger tea. Steep four or five slices of fresh ginger in hot water for ten minutes. Add a squeeze of lemon.
  3. Overnight oats. Stir half a cup of oats and a spoonful of chia seeds into a cup of oat or almond milk, and leave it in the fridge overnight. Top with berries and walnuts in the morning. No chopping needed.
  4. Cherry and berry smoothie. Blend a cup of frozen cherries and berries with a banana and a cup of oat milk or plant yogurt. Thirty seconds, and no knife.
  5. White bean smash on toast. Mash a can of drained white beans with lemon, garlic and black pepper. Pile it onto wholegrain toast with sliced tomato and a sprinkle of ground flaxseed. Protein and omega-3s in five minutes.
  6. One-tray chickpeas. Toss a can of drained chickpeas and some chopped vegetables on a baking tray with olive oil, lemon, garlic and a pinch of turmeric. Bake at 200°C (400°F) for 20 to 25 minutes.
  7. Red lentil soup. Simmer a cup of red lentils with a chopped onion, a carrot, a teaspoon of turmeric and four cups of vegetable stock for 25 minutes. It freezes well for hard days.
  8. Olive oil vegetable bowl. Roast whatever vegetables you have, spoon them over brown rice and chickpeas, and finish with olive oil and lemon.

Go easy on your hands. Buy pre-chopped or frozen vegetables without a second thought. Use a jar opener and a wide-handled knife. Cook double and freeze half, so a meal is waiting on the days your joints need a rest.

When to see a doctor

Taking care of yourself includes knowing when to bring someone else in. If you don't have a diagnosis yet, start there. And please don't wait on any of the signs below.

Get care the same day

  • One joint suddenly turns hot, red and very painful, especially with a fever. This can be a joint infection.
  • A joint can't take your weight after a fall or injury.

Book an appointment within a few weeks

  • Swelling in several joints, or morning stiffness that lasts more than an hour.
  • Joint pain along with a rash, tiredness, weight loss or eye problems.
  • Pain that wakes you at night or keeps getting worse.

Treatments that work alongside natural care

  • Osteoarthritis: anti-inflammatory gels are strongly recommended for knees and are easy on the rest of your body. Tablets, steroid injections and, when pain and movement call for it, joint replacement are there if you need them.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: medicines that calm the disease itself, like methotrexate. Starting early is what prevents lasting joint damage.
  • Gout: medicine that lowers uric acid, like allopurinol, if flares keep coming back, usually aiming for a level below 6 mg/dL.

Using pain relievers safely

  • Anti-inflammatory tablets like ibuprofen and naproxen help many people. Taken every day, though, they can cause stomach ulcers or bleeding, raise blood pressure and strain the kidneys. Take them with food, and ask your doctor about protecting your stomach, especially if you're over 65 or have had stomach bleeding before. They also make your body hold on to water, so go easy on salt, Celtic salt included, while you're taking them.
  • Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is easier on the stomach, but too much of it, or taking it with alcohol, can harm the liver. Stay within the dose on the label.
  • Change, add or stop a pain reliever only after speaking with your doctor.

Conditions that can look like arthritis

Some illnesses show up as joint pain before anything else. Lyme disease can follow a tick bite, sometimes with a spreading rash and flu-like aches, and it's treated with antibiotics. Lupus and inflammatory bowel disease, such as ulcerative colitis, can also start with sore joints. If your joint pain came along with any of these, tell your doctor.

Tell your doctor and pharmacist about every herb and supplement you take.

Your weekly action plan

The most studied idea in arthritis self-care isn't a food or a stretch. It's believing you can manage your arthritis, then proving it to yourself one small week at a time.

Researchers at Stanford call it self-efficacy. In their long-running studies, building that belief did more for people's health than the changes in their habits did. It's the heart of The Arthritis Helpbook by Kate Lorig and James Fries, and it's simple to use.

How to write one

  1. Pick one small, specific thing. Instead of "exercise more," try "walk for ten minutes after lunch on Monday, Wednesday and Friday."
  2. Ask yourself how sure you are that you'll do it, from 0 to 10.
  3. If your answer is below 7, make the plan smaller until it's 7 or more.
  4. At the end of the week, look at how it went, and write next week's plan.

Your pain toolbox

  • Warmth before you get active. A wrapped cold pack on a hot, swollen joint, for up to 20 minutes.
  • Slow breathing, distraction and mindfulness, which change how your brain handles pain signals.
  • Massage from a trained therapist.
  • Your medicines as prescribed, including anti-inflammatory gels for hands and knees.

When tiredness is the problem

  • Split big jobs into short blocks with rests between them, and save the hard ones for your best time of day.
  • Keep a regular bedtime, and ease your pain before you go to bed.
  • Moving regularly builds energy over a few weeks, even though rest feels better in the moment.
  • In rheumatoid or other inflammatory arthritis, deep tiredness can mean the disease is active. Tell your doctor.

Questions people often ask

How soon will I feel a difference?

Capsaicin cream usually takes two to four weeks. Exercise often eases stiffness within a few weeks, and helps more over two to three months. Be patient with yourself.

Can I do this instead of taking my medicine?

Only after speaking with your doctor. Everything here is meant to work alongside your treatment. If you'd like to take less medicine, your doctor can lower it safely as you improve, and a good tracker makes that conversation easier.

Exercise hurts. Should I stop?

Mild soreness that settles within a day is safe and expected. Sharp pain, or pain that hangs around, means do less next time. Less, not nothing.

Will exercise wear out my joints?

No. Sensible movement feeds your cartilage and strengthens the muscles that protect it. Stillness is what joints should worry about.

Is cracking or creaking a bad sign?

Usually not. Joints that click without pain or swelling are just noisy.

Does the weather really affect arthritis?

Lots of people feel worse in cold, damp weather, and you're not imagining it. Stay warm, keep moving, and know it passes when the weather does.

Are these remedies safe with my other health conditions?

Mostly, yes. Check turmeric and ginger supplements with a pharmacist if you take blood thinners or diabetes medicine, and keep capsaicin cream off broken skin.

Can my arthritis go away completely?

Some kinds can. Joint infections and Lyme arthritis clear up with the right antibiotics, and gout flares can stop for good once uric acid is kept low. Most people with rheumatoid arthritis who are treated early now reach remission, which means no active disease. With osteoarthritis, pain and stiffness often improve so much that life opens up again. "Can arthritis be reversed?" in Part one has the details.

Am I too old to start?

You're not. People in their seventies, eighties and nineties get stronger and move more easily with exercise. The best day to start is today.

I've tried everything and I'm tired. Is it worth it?

Your tiredness makes sense. You don't have to try everything again. Pick the smallest step in week one and start there. That's enough for today.

Other voices worth hearing

Barbara O'Neill isn't the only person with a following for natural arthritis care. If you spend time in the library, you've probably come across a few of these names too. Here's what each one teaches, and what's worth taking from it.

Most of them agree with doctors on the basics: move every day, eat more plants, keep your weight down, sleep well and ease your stress. Where they part ways is when they suggest medicine can be dropped. On that point the evidence is mostly personal stories.

Clint Paddison and the Paddison Program

Only with your doctor

Paddison built his program after living with rheumatoid arthritis himself. It's a mostly plant-based diet that drops meat, dairy, oil and processed food to settle the gut and calm inflammation. It starts with a two-day cleanse, then about two weeks of very plain "baseline" eating, and then foods come back one at a time. Exercise and managing stress are part of it too.

Arthritis New Zealand shared the story of one member who followed it while staying on methotrexate. She said she was about 90% pain-free after six months, with no active inflammation found, and that the plain baseline weeks were the hardest part. The charity's own view is blunt: it's "not an evidence-based diet recommended for any condition." If you have rheumatoid arthritis, your medicine is what prevents lasting joint damage, so talk to your doctor before starting a program like this and keep taking what you've been prescribed.

Dr. Susan Blum, Healing Arthritis

Yours to try

Dr. Blum is a physician who has lived with an autoimmune condition herself. Her book looks for the root causes of inflammation in the gut, in food and in stress, and readers like how practical it is. It hasn't been tested as a program in large trials. The habits it asks for, though, like more plants, less processed food, better sleep and less stress, overlap with what this book already recommends.

Dr. D.C. Jarvis, Arthritis and Folk Medicine (1960)

Relax about this one

A Vermont country doctor who gathered the remedies of local farm families. His book made apple cider vinegar famous as an arthritis drink, and people still pass it around today. No trials show it helps joints. If you enjoy it, dilute it well and drink it through a straw, because the acid is hard on your teeth. Skip it if you have reflux.

Dr. Collin Dong, New Hope for the Arthritic (1975)

Relax about this one

Dr. Dong cut out dairy, fruit, red meat and chocolate, believing chemicals and allergies in food set arthritis off. At the time, the Arthritis Foundation said diet had nothing to do with it. The truth sits somewhere in between. Food matters a great deal in gout and for weight, and some people do have their own trigger foods. Part two shows you a safe way to find yours.

What they all have in common

Look past the differences and the same habits turn up in every one of these books: caring for the whole person, using food as medicine, moving every day, resting and easing stress, and taking charge of your own care. Those are also the habits with the most research behind them, so that's where to put your energy first.

Extra reading

Three hundred years of remedies

Long before supplements and phone apps, people with "rheumatism" and gout were trying lemons, celery, milk diets and hot baths. Some of what they found still holds up. Plenty doesn't.

For about 300 years, writers blamed rheumatism and gout on eating too much, on too much meat, and on "poisons" building up in the joints. They prescribed plain food, fruit, vegetables and patience. A few of their books are now free to read online, and they show how people thought.

A milk diet for gout (1700s)

A European physician named Dolæus wrote that a strict, simple milk diet could cure gout. His English translator, William Stephens, said the real value of the book was its case histories. Nobody uses milk-only diets today.

The lemon cure (1908)

In Food Remedies, Florence Daniel recommended lemon juice in hot water, morning and night. The full "lemon cure" climbed from one lemon a day to twelve, and Daniel herself thought that went too far. She called celery "almost a specific" for rheumatism and gout, and she praised potatoes steamed in their skins, with the cooking water used to bathe swollen joints.

She believed fruit acids "purify the blood." That part hasn't survived. The plate full of vegetables has.

Earthy deposits (early 1900s)

Henry Lindlahr's Nature Cure taught that glue-like, "earthy" deposits build up in rheumatic joints, and that natural living dissolves them. You can hear the same idea a century later when Barbara O'Neill talks about breaking up calcification. No study has shown that food, poultices or oils dissolve bone spurs. Gout crystals can dissolve, but it's medicine that lowers uric acid that does it.

Hope for your arthritis (1948)

This booklet told readers that nine out of ten patients could expect some benefit, and that more than 85% of deformities could be prevented. The hope, it said, came from earlier diagnosis, better nutrition and patients who stuck with their care, not from any miracle drug. It warned against "overnight cures." Nearly 80 years later, that advice still stands.

What held up, and what didn't

  • Held up: gout really is linked to rich food, red meat and alcohol.
  • Held up: fruit and vegetables really are linked to lower risk.
  • Held up: catching it early really does prevent deformity.
  • Didn't hold up: "blood poisons," cures from lemons alone, and milk-only diets.
