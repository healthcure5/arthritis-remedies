## Why the answer is often no {#why}

Pain, for one, and the worry that moving will hurt more. Fear of falling can drive reluctance too.[^1] Pride is another. Nobody likes being supervised. The Arthritis Foundation reminds families that pain isn't always visible, even on days a loved one seems fine, and that comparing it to someone else's doesn't help.[^2]

## What helps, in small steps {#small}

AARP's advice for motivating a less active older adult is simple and kind:[^1]

- **Start small.** A little movement at first. Each bit builds toward lasting benefit.
- **Choose what they enjoy.** Pair movement with a favorite podcast, audiobook or TV show, like pedaling a stationary bike while listening.
- **Make it social.** Regular walks, a yoga class or gardening with friends or neighbors.
- **Join them.** Do it together, such as chair tai chi, so they have someone to copy.
- **Build it into the day.** Chair squats after meals, toe taps while the coffee brews, marching in place during commercials.
- **Let them pick the outing.** Gardens, museums or a farmers market, with some walking built in.
- **Bring in the grandchildren.** Gentle games like cornhole or freeze dance.
- **Point out the good.** "You seem brighter after that walk."
- **Keep it light.** Staying patient, calm and lighthearted avoids a power struggle.
- **Allow aids.** A cane or rollator on walks is fine, since fear of falling often drives reluctance.

The Arthritis Foundation adds that you can suggest low-impact options like walks or swims in a heated pool, sign up for a yoga or tai chi class together, and adjust plans on days when pain flares.[^2]

## Let a professional say it {#pro}

Some older adults are skeptical of family advice but listen to a doctor or physical therapist.[^1] The CDC suggests that people talk to a health care provider before starting, and a physical therapist can help them begin.[^3] You can offer to drive them or sit in on the appointment.[^2]

## And if it's still a no {#no}

Keep checking in, and let them know you're ready when they are. The Arthritis Foundation puts it well: stay persistent, even when they don't want help or company.[^2] Look after yourself too. @faq
Q: Why won't my parent with arthritis exercise?
A: Common reasons are fear of pain, fear of falling, and not wanting to be told what to do. Starting very small and choosing something they enjoy usually works better than pushing.
Q: How can I motivate an elderly parent to move?
A: AARP suggests starting small, pairing movement with something they enjoy, doing it together, building it into daily routines and keeping things lighthearted.
Q: What exercises are safe for an older parent with arthritis?
A: Low-impact choices like walking, tai chi, swimming or a heated-pool class, and chair exercises. A doctor or physical therapist can pick moves that fit their joints.
Q: Should I nag my mom about her arthritis exercises?
A: Nagging tends to cause power struggles. Offer a specific, easy plan, like "walk with me Saturday," and let a doctor or physical therapist reinforce it.
Q: How can I help a parent with arthritis day to day?
A: Ask what they need most, offer specific help like groceries or rides, respect their independence, and take their pain seriously even on good days.
