When Mom has arthritis: a field guide for grown children

Watching Mom or Dad wince getting out of a chair is hard. Here are practical ways to make their days easier, and to take care of yourself.

The Hinge Care team
Updated November 18, 2026 · ☀ Every fact linked to a source below

Hinge Care has one aim: less arthritis pain, less stiffness, and more good days for the people living with it.

In short

  • Start by asking your parent what's hardest, and listen before fixing.
  • Small home changes matter: items at waist height, easy-grip tools, grab bars, fewer trip hazards.
  • Help them stay active. The CDC says activity can cut arthritis pain by about 40%.
  • Come to appointments, keep a medicine list, and look after yourself too.

It starts small. Mom takes the stairs one at a time now. Dad lets you open the pickle jar without the usual joke. Then one day you watch your parent grip the arm of a chair to stand up, and something in your chest goes tight.

You can't take their arthritis away. You can make their days easier, and that counts for a lot. Start with how you talk about it.

What to say, and what to skip

Before you buy gadgets or book appointments, find out what is actually hardest. The Arthritis Foundation's advice for families starts there: learn about the condition and listen to the person living with it.1 Many parents play down their pain so their children won't worry, so the way you ask matters.

Try sayingInstead of
"What's been the hardest part this week?""Are you okay?"
"Want to walk to the mailbox with me?""You need to exercise more."
"Which jobs would be easiest for me to take off your plate?""Let me do everything."
"Shall I come to the next appointment and take notes?""Did the doctor say anything?"

Room by room

Small changes around the house add up. Walk through it with your parent and ask what feels awkward.

Bedroom

  • Water and glasses by the bed
  • A night light on the way to the bathroom
  • Clothes laid out the night before

Bathroom

  • Grab bars by the toilet and shower
  • A raised toilet seat
  • A shower stool

Kitchen

  • Mugs, plates and kettle at waist height
  • A jar opener and wide-grip utensils
  • Easy-open pill bottle caps from the pharmacist

Hallways and living room

  • Loose rugs and cords cleared away
  • Lever door handles
  • A firm chair with arms to push up from

Help them keep moving

It's natural to want to do everything for a parent in pain. But staying active is one of the best things for arthritis. The CDC says adults with arthritis can cut pain and improve function by about 40% by being physically active.2

Invite them for a short walk, or try a few chair exercises together. Our five-minute chair routine has six you can do side by side.

Doctor visits and medicines

  • Offer to come to appointments and take notes. Write questions down beforehand.
  • Keep a simple list of every medicine and supplement, with doses, to bring to each visit.
  • Notice changes: new swelling, a joint that's hot or red, falls, or pain that keeps them awake. Tell the doctor.
  • Never change a prescribed medicine yourself. Any change should only happen after speaking with their doctor.

Look after yourself, too

Caregiving wears people down quietly. According to an AARP survey cited by the Arthritis Foundation, the average family caregiver is a woman in her late 40s caring for an aging parent while also raising a family and working.3

Share the load with siblings, take short breaks without guilt, and consider a caregiver support group. Looking after yourself is how you keep showing up.

Questions people ask about arthritis

How can I help my elderly parent with arthritis?

Start by asking what's hardest for them. Then make small home changes, like moving everyday items to waist height and adding grab bars, help them stay active, and offer to come to doctor visits.

What makes arthritis easier for seniors at home?

Easy-grip utensils, jar openers, lever door handles, a shower stool, grab bars, a raised toilet seat, and keeping floors clear of rugs and cords all make daily life easier.

Should my parent with arthritis rest or exercise?

Regular activity usually helps more than rest. The CDC says being active can reduce arthritis pain and improve function. Rest a joint that's hot, red or very swollen, and ask the doctor.

How do I talk to my parent about their arthritis pain?

Choose a calm moment, ask open questions like 'What's been the hardest part this week?', and listen without rushing to fix it. Regular, low-pressure check-ins help them be honest.

What should I buy a parent who has arthritis?

Practical comfort gifts are often the most loved: a heated throw, an electric jar opener, easy-grip kitchen tools, or a program or book that helps them feel more in control of their mornings.

How do I avoid caregiver burnout?

Share tasks with siblings or friends, take regular breaks, keep up with your own health, and consider a caregiver support group. You can't pour from an empty cup.
