How to help a parent with arthritis

Watching Mom or Dad struggle with stiff, sore joints is hard. Here are practical ways to make their days easier, and to take care of yourself.

Key points

  • Start by asking your parent what's hardest, and listen before fixing.
  • Small home changes matter: items at waist height, easy-grip tools, grab bars, fewer trip hazards.
  • Help them stay active. The CDC says activity can cut arthritis pain by about 40%.
  • Come to appointments, keep a medicine list, and look after yourself too.

It starts small. Mom takes the stairs one at a time now. Dad lets you open the pickle jar without the usual joke. Then one day you watch your parent grip the arm of a chair to stand up, and something in your chest goes tight.

You can't take their arthritis away. But you can make their days easier, and your support matters more than you might think. Here's where to start.

Start by listening, not fixing

Before you buy gadgets or book appointments, ask your parent what's hardest. Is it mornings? Buttons? Getting out of the car? The Arthritis Foundation's advice for families starts in the same place: learn about the condition and listen to the person living with it.1

Ask "What's been the hardest part this week?" Then let them answer without jumping in.

Many parents play down their pain so their children won't worry. A calm, regular check-in makes it easier for them to be honest.

Small changes around the house

  1. Make the morning easierWater by the bed, clothes laid out the night before, and a shower stool. Our article on morning joint stiffness has more ideas.
  2. Move everyday items to waist heightMugs, plates, the kettle and favorite pans should sit where your parent doesn't have to reach high or bend low.
  3. Swap tricky handles and lidsLever door handles, jar openers, and wide-grip utensils save sore hands. Ask the pharmacist for easy-open bottle caps.
  4. Clear trip hazardsLoose rugs, cords and clutter on the floor are risky for stiff hips and knees. Add a night light between the bed and the bathroom.
  5. Add grab bars and a raised seatGrab bars by the toilet and in the shower, and a raised toilet seat, make standing up much easier.

Help them keep moving

It's natural to want to do everything for a parent in pain. But staying active is one of the best things for arthritis. The CDC says adults with arthritis can cut pain and improve function by about 40% by being physically active.2

Invite them for a short walk, or try a few chair exercises together. Our guide to gentle exercises for arthritis in seniors has six you can do side by side.

Doctor visits and medicines

  • Offer to come to appointments and take notes. Write questions down beforehand.
  • Keep a simple list of every medicine and supplement, with doses, to bring to each visit.
  • Notice changes: new swelling, a joint that's hot or red, falls, or pain that keeps them awake. Tell the doctor.
  • Never change a prescribed medicine yourself. Any change should only happen after speaking with their doctor.

Look after yourself, too

If you're stretched thin, you're not alone. According to an AARP survey cited by the Arthritis Foundation, the average family caregiver is a woman in her late 40s caring for an aging parent while also raising a family and working.3

Share the load with siblings, take short breaks without guilt, and consider a caregiver support group. Looking after yourself is how you keep showing up.

Questions people ask

How can I help my elderly parent with arthritis?

Start by asking what's hardest for them. Then make small home changes, like moving everyday items to waist height and adding grab bars, help them stay gently active, and offer to come to doctor visits.

What makes arthritis easier for seniors at home?

Easy-grip utensils, jar openers, lever door handles, a shower stool, grab bars, a raised toilet seat, and keeping floors clear of rugs and cords all make daily life easier.

Should my parent with arthritis rest or exercise?

Gentle, regular activity usually helps more than rest. The CDC says being active can reduce arthritis pain and improve function. Rest a joint that's hot, red or very swollen, and ask the doctor.

How do I talk to my parent about their arthritis pain?

Choose a calm moment, ask open questions like 'What's been the hardest part this week?', and listen without rushing to fix it. Regular, low-pressure check-ins help them be honest.

What is a good gift for a parent with arthritis?

Practical comfort gifts are often the most loved: a heated throw, an electric jar opener, easy-grip kitchen tools, or a gentle program or book that helps them feel more in control of their mornings.

How do I avoid caregiver burnout?

Share tasks with siblings or friends, take regular breaks, keep up with your own health, and consider a caregiver support group. You can't pour from an empty cup.
